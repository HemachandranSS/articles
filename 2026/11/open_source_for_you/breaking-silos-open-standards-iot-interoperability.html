Breaking the Silos: How Open Standards Enable IoT Interoperability

To be truly interoperable on a large scale, IoT devices must communicate using open standards. Else, their utility will be restricted to small islands of connectivity.

By Dr Mohit

Open Source For You  |  November 2026  |  Pages 46–49

The Internet of Things was meant to let devices—smart meters, solar panels, factory machines, medical sensors—talk to each other. In practice, billions of devices today sit in small islands of connectivity, or silos, because vendors use proprietary radios, data formats and clouds. Scaling projects across those islands is costly and fragile.

Open standards such as MQTT, CoAP and OPC UA give devices a shared language. They are free to use and easy to adopt with mature open-source tools.

What interoperability means

Interoperability is the ability of different systems to interact, exchange information and apply it appropriately. It is useful to think in four levels:

  1. Technical — ability to connect (Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, Zigbee, LoRaWAN, Ethernet, and so on).
  2. Syntactic — ability to exchange messages with agreed formats and protocols (JSON, CBOR, MQTT, CoAP).
  3. Semantic — shared meaning of data (is “25” Celsius or Fahrenheit?).
  4. Organisational — safe sharing under mutual agreements, including security and access rules between companies.

How are open standards useful?

IoT devices often fail to communicate for five recurring reasons:

  1. Excessive radios and networks — Wi-Fi, Zigbee, Z-Wave, BLE, LoRaWAN, NB-IoT and others each move data differently.
  2. Many data formats — JSON, raw bytes or vendor-specific binary; even with JSON, field names often disagree.
  3. Closed vendor clouds — proprietary apps and clouds trap customers and make migration hard.
  4. Old industrial systems — machines 20–30 years old still speak Modbus or proprietary fieldbus.
  5. Small, low-power devices — sensors with limited RAM/CPU cannot run heavy server protocols.

The benefits of open standards

An open standard is a technical rule book maintained by a neutral body (OASIS, IETF, IEC, and so on) that anyone can use without licence fees. Four benefits stand out:

  1. A shared language — devices from different vendors can communicate.
  2. No lock-in — you can switch vendors without abandoning the standard.
  3. Available open-source tools — popular standards ship with mature codebases.
  4. A large community — security bugs are found and fixed quickly.

Without open standards, every device–app pair needs its own custom glue. With a common open standard (MQTT / CoAP / OPC UA), each device and app connects once.

MQTT: The common messenger

Message Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT) is a lightweight messaging protocol developed in 1999 to track pipelines over slow satellite links. Version 3.1.1 is an OASIS standard and ISO/IEC 20922; MQTT 5.0 is the current major version.

It uses a publish/subscribe model via a central broker. Sensors publish to a topic (for example, farm/field1/soil-moisture); apps subscribe; the broker delivers to all subscribers. The smallest message header is just 2 bytes, over TCP.

Quality of Service:

  • QoS 0 (at most once) — quick; message may be lost.
  • QoS 1 (at least once) — delivered, but may arrive twice.
  • QoS 2 (exactly once) — safest but slowest.

Popular open-source brokers include Eclipse Mosquitto, EMQX and VerneMQ; Eclipse Paho provides clients for C, Python, Java, JavaScript and more.

CoAP: The web for tiny devices

Constrained Application Protocol (CoAP) is an IETF standard protocol for very light devices on weak networks. It is similar to HTTP, but smaller: resources live at URLs such as coap://sensor1/temperature, with GET, POST, PUT and DELETE. It runs over UDP (non-blocking) rather than TCP, with a 4-byte fixed header, and supports an Observe option for subscriptions.

Open-source implementations include libcoap (C), Eclipse Californium (Java) and aiocoap (Python). CoAP is also built into embedded OSes such as Zephyr, RIOT and Contiki-NG.

OPC UA: The language of the factory

Open Platform Communications Unified Architecture is managed by the OPC Foundation and published as IEC 62541. It is widely used in factories and industrial automation.

MQTT and CoAP mainly solve the syntactic level—they carry messages but do not define meaning. OPC UA also addresses the semantic level through an information model: each machine describes what it is, which values it exposes, their units and how they relate (for example, a pump’s speed, pressure and temperature). A new client can browse a machine without reading a vendor handbook. Industry groups publish companion specifications for robots, machine tools, packaging lines and more.

OPC UA supports encryption, signing and certificate-based authentication. Recent versions add publish/subscribe that can run on top of MQTT. Open-source implementations include open62541 (C), Eclipse Milo (Java) and Python’s asyncua.

The gateway can read CoAP, OPC UA or even old Modbus, normalise the data and publish to an MQTT broker so apps subscribe to topics and never see the original device protocol. Other open standards

  • LwM2M (Lightweight M2M) — from OMA SpecWorks on top of CoAP; remote device administration (settings, firmware, health).
  • SenML (Sensor Measurement Lists) — RFC 8428; simple JSON/CBOR sensor readings with names and units so “25 Cel” means the same everywhere.
  • Matter — Connectivity Standards Alliance (2022); lets smart-home devices from Apple, Google, Amazon, Samsung and others interoperate; open reference code.
  • AMQP — OASIS/ISO reliable business messaging; often used from IoT gateway into enterprise systems.

Closing

Devices that can work together are key to IoT. Closed systems produce islands. Open standards are the bridges that unite them. MQTT provides a lightweight way to transport data; CoAP brings the web to the smallest devices; OPC UA adds meaning and security for industrial machines. Together with gateways and common data models, they form a solid open stack—free and easy to adopt with Mosquitto, Paho, libcoap, open62541 and related tools. The message for students, startups and industry is clear: communicate using open standards, and your IoT system will be able to speak with the world.
