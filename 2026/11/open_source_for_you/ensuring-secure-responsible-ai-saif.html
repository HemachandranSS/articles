Ensuring Secure and Responsible AI with SAIF

The Secure AI Framework (SAIF) gives organisations a practical foundation for building secure and governable AI systems.

By Dr Magesh Kasthuri and Dr Anand Nayyar

Open Source For You  |  November 2026  |  Pages 83–89

Artificial intelligence is used today to automate operations, support customer engagement, improve engineering productivity, detect cyber threats, personalise services, and generate business insights at scale. This rapid adoption creates enormous value, but it also introduces new risks that traditional IT governance models were not designed to manage. Models can be manipulated through malicious prompts, sensitive data may leak through outputs, training data can be poisoned, and autonomous agents may perform unintended actions if controls are weak. The Secure AI Framework, commonly known as SAIF, provides a practical way to address these challenges by extending proven security principles into the AI lifecycle.

SAIF was introduced by Google as a conceptual framework for securing AI systems and is built around the idea that AI should be secure by default, resilient by design, and governed throughout its lifecycle. The framework is not limited to a single technology stack or cloud platform. Instead, it offers a structured security and governance lens that organisations can apply across data, infrastructure, models, applications, assurance practices, and executive oversight. In simple terms, SAIF helps an enterprise ask the right questions before, during, and after an AI solution is deployed: What data is being used? Who can access the model? How are prompts and outputs monitored? What happens when the system behaves unexpectedly? Who is accountable for approving, funding, securing, and operating the AI capability?

Why SAIF matters for AI governance

AI governance is the discipline of directing, controlling, and continuously improving the way AI is developed and used. It connects business objectives with ethical expectations, regulatory obligations, cybersecurity controls, operational resilience, and measurable accountability. Without governance, AI initiatives often grow in fragmented ways. One team may build a chatbot using customer data, another may create a predictive model for operations, and a third may experiment with generative AI for software delivery. Each initiative may be useful, but the organisation may lack a common policy for data handling, access control, model validation, human review, auditability, and incident response.

SAIF strengthens AI governance by converting security and risk thinking into repeatable processes. It encourages organisations to define guardrails before AI moves into production, align AI risk with enterprise risk management, and create ownership across technology, security, legal, finance, strategy, and business teams. It also makes governance more practical. Rather than treating AI ethics, security, privacy, compliance, and operations as separate conversations, SAIF brings them into a shared operating model. This helps leaders avoid two common extremes: uncontrolled innovation on one side and excessive bureaucracy on the other.

How SAIF helps an organisation build governance and process

SAIF helps organisations move from informal AI experimentation to disciplined AI delivery. First, it creates a common vocabulary for AI risk. Teams can discuss prompt injection, data leakage, model theft, adversarial behaviour, hallucination risk, privacy exposure, and agentic misuse without treating them as isolated technical concerns. Second, it embeds security into each stage of the AI lifecycle, from use-case intake and data selection to model testing, deployment, monitoring, and retirement. Third, it clarifies decision rights. Leaders can decide which AI systems require executive approval, which controls are mandatory, and which risks must be accepted formally.

A SAIF-based process typically begins with an AI use-case assessment. The business sponsor explains the intended value, the AI head assesses feasibility, the chief security officer evaluates threat exposure, the CIO confirms platform readiness, and the legal or compliance team reviews regulatory implications. After that, the organisation defines control requirements such as identity and access management, data classification, model evaluation, human oversight, secure logging, output filtering, red-team testing, and incident escalation. IDC’s research on AI security highlights that data control and identity management are two foundational pillars for securing generative AI adoption, which aligns closely with this SAIF-based control sequence. Once the AI system is live, SAIF continues to guide monitoring, improvement, and periodic reassessment.

Core building blocks of SAIF

Expanding strong security foundations to the AI ecosystem: The first building block is about extending existing security foundations into AI. Most organisations already have controls for identity, network security, data protection, vulnerability management, application security, and secure software development. SAIF asks enterprises to apply these controls to AI-specific assets such as training datasets, feature stores, model weights, prompt templates, vector databases, AI APIs, and agent toolchains. For example, a bank deploying a generative AI assistant for relationship managers should not allow unrestricted access to customer records. The same principles used for core banking security—least privilege, encryption, audit logging, segregation of duties, and data masking—must also govern the AI assistant.

Extending detection and response to AI threats: Traditional cyber monitoring focuses on endpoints, networks, applications, identities, and cloud workloads. AI introduces additional signals that must be observed. These include unusual prompt patterns, repeated attempts to extract sensitive information, abnormal model responses, unexpected tool invocation by agents, and suspicious access to model artifacts. Consider a customer-support chatbot used by a telecom provider. If attackers repeatedly ask the chatbot to ignore previous instructions or reveal internal policy documents, the security team must be able to detect this behaviour quickly. SAIF encourages organisations to bring AI applications into the enterprise threat universe so that AI incidents are monitored, triaged, and handled with the same seriousness as conventional cyber incidents.

Automating defences to keep pace with new threats: AI threats can scale quickly, and manual controls alone are rarely sufficient. SAIF recommends automation wherever it improves speed, consistency, and resilience. Automated defences may include real-time prompt filtering, data loss prevention checks, toxicity and safety classifiers, model behaviour monitoring, security rule generation, anomaly detection, and automated quarantine of suspicious workflows. For example, in a software engineering environment, a coding assistant may generate code snippets that accidentally include insecure patterns. An automated security scanner can inspect generated code, flag vulnerable dependencies, and route high-risk findings to a human reviewer. This does not remove human accountability; rather, it helps human experts focus on the decisions that require judgment.

Harmonising platform-level controls across the organisation: Many enterprises use multiple AI platforms, cloud providers, open source models, commercial APIs, and internal development environments. If each team defines its own controls, the organisation soon faces inconsistent practices and uneven risk exposure. SAIF promotes harmonised platform-level controls so that AI projects follow common standards regardless of where they are built. IDC has described unified AI governance platforms as integrated capabilities that support model inventory, risk controls, continuous monitoring, policy management, and audit readiness across traditional machine learning, generative AI, and agentic AI. For instance, a healthcare organisation may use one platform for clinical summarisation, another for claims automation, and a third for research analytics. Even if the technical stacks differ, the governance controls should remain consistent: approved model registry, classified datasets, access reviews, audit trails, secure APIs, human validation for high-impact decisions, and documented risk acceptance.

Adapting controls and creating faster feedback loops: AI systems are not static. Models are updated, prompts are refined, user behaviour changes, and attackers discover new techniques. SAIF therefore emphasises adaptive controls and fast feedback loops. Organisations should conduct red-team exercises, review model outputs, track incidents, collect user feedback, and adjust policies when risk patterns change. An example is an insurance company using AI to summarise claims documents. If reviewers notice that the model occasionally omits critical exclusions or misinterprets handwritten notes, the feedback should trigger improvement actions such as retraining, prompt revision, better document-quality checks, or mandatory human validation for complex claims. Governance becomes stronger when operational evidence continuously improves the control environment.

Contextualising AI risk within business processes: The final building block is perhaps the most important for governance. AI risk cannot be assessed only at the model level; it must be understood in the business context where the model operates. A low-risk model used for internal document classification is very different from an AI system that recommends credit decisions, guides medical triage, or executes supplier payments. SAIF encourages leaders to connect technical controls with business impact, customer trust, legal obligations, financial exposure, and reputational risk. For example, an AI procurement agent that can create purchase orders must have spending limits, approval workflows, segregation of duties, and exception handling. The risk is not merely that the model may produce an incorrect answer but that the business process may act on it without sufficient control.

Practical operating model for SAIF adoption

A practical SAIF operating model should begin with executive sponsorship and end with measurable control assurance. The CEO sets the tone that AI must be innovative, secure, ethical, and aligned with enterprise strategy. The CTO and AI head shape the technical architecture, model lifecycle, engineering practices, and AI enablement roadmap. The CIO ensures that AI platforms integrate with enterprise systems, service management, data platforms, and operational support. The chief security officer defines the threat model, security controls, monitoring approach, and incident response playbooks. The CFO validates funding, cost governance, value tracking, and financial risk. The chief strategy officer ensures that AI adoption supports the organisation’s competitive direction and long-term transformation priorities.

The process should include an AI intake board, risk classification, data review, model approval, security architecture review, red-team testing, release readiness assessment, production monitoring, periodic audit, and retirement procedures. This may sound extensive, but it should be scaled based on risk. A low-impact internal productivity tool may require a lighter review, while a customer-facing AI system that influences financial or operational decisions should go through a more rigorous control path. The strength of SAIF lies in this balance: it does not slow AI unnecessarily, but it ensures that speed is supported by discipline.

How SAIF works with popular AI solutions

SAIF can be applied across popular AI solutions because it is designed as a control framework rather than a product-specific architecture. Whether an enterprise uses Google Vertex AI, Microsoft Azure AI and Azure OpenAI Service, AWS Bedrock, Amazon SageMaker, OpenAI enterprise APIs, Anthropic Claude, IBM watsonx, Databricks Mosaic AI, Snowflake Cortex AI, Hugging Face Enterprise Hub, Nvidia AI Enterprise, Kubernetes-based MLOps platforms, or on-premises model deployments, the central governance questions remain consistent: What data enters the system? Who can access the model? How are outputs controlled? How are risks monitored? How is accountability maintained? SAIF functions as a unifying layer that maps enterprise security controls to AI-specific workflows.

In generative AI and large language model deployments, SAIF begins with strong identity, access, and data controls. Users, applications, service accounts, APIs, vector databases, prompt templates, and model endpoints should be governed through least privilege, role-based or attribute-based access control, encryption, secret management, audit logging, and policy enforcement. For example, a retrieval-augmented generation system built on Azure OpenAI or AWS Bedrock should not simply connect a model to enterprise documents. The retrieval layer must enforce document-level authorisation, data classification, query filtering, vector index governance, query attribution, and response validation. Otherwise, the system may expose sensitive records even if the underlying model is secure.

For traditional machine learning platforms such as SageMaker, Vertex AI, Databricks, or watsonx, SAIF aligns with MLOps controls: secure data pipelines, model lineage, feature store governance, training-data validation, model approval gates, vulnerability scanning of containers, signed artifacts, model registry controls, deployment approval, and continuous monitoring for drift or abnormal predictions. For genAI platforms, the control surface expands to prompt injection protection, output moderation, jailbreak detection, hallucination monitoring, adversarial testing, red teaming, and data loss prevention across prompts and responses. AI copilots and agents require additional safeguards because they may invoke tools, call APIs, generate code, modify documents, or trigger business workflows. In these cases, SAIF requires constrained permissions, tool-level authorisation, approval workflows, transaction limits, and clear human-in-the-loop checkpoints.

The same logic applies to computer vision systems, predictive analytics, customer support bots, coding assistants, and autonomous workflow agents. SAIF does not ask organisations to abandon platform-native controls; instead, it harmonises them with enterprise security architecture, zero trust principles, governance risk and compliance processes, and responsible AI expectations. Cloud security posture management, API gateways, SIEM and SOAR integration, confidential computing, model monitoring, and incident response should all extend into the AI environment. This makes SAIF especially valuable in hybrid enterprises where multiple AI solutions coexist.

Future trends

Current AI governance is still heavily focused on use case approval, data protection, prompt controls, and model monitoring. These controls remain necessary, but they will not be sufficient as AI systems begin to plan tasks, use tools, interact with enterprise applications, generate software, process video and audio, and coordinate multi-step workflows. SAIF will therefore need to evolve from a lifecycle framework into a continuous assurance model that can observe and govern AI behaviour at runtime.

One major trend is the rise of autonomous AI agents and agentic workflows. Future SAIF implementations will need stronger controls for agent identity, tool permissions, memory management, transaction approval, sandboxed execution, and policy-based orchestration. Runtime guardrails will become as important as pre-deployment review. Enterprises will need to know not only whether an AI model was approved, but also what the agent did, which tools it called, what data it accessed, and whether its actions remained within business policy.

Another trend is the growing importance of AI supply-chain security. Organisations will increasingly require model provenance, dataset lineage, signed model artifacts, AI bills of materials, secure model registries, vulnerability tracking for open source models, and evidence that third-party AI services meet enterprise security and compliance requirements. Watermarking, content authenticity, synthetic data governance, and output traceability will become more relevant as AI-generated content enters regulated workflows. Privacy-preserving machine learning, federated learning, confidential AI, and trusted execution environments may also become more common where sensitive data cannot be freely centralised or exposed to external model providers.

Regulatory and standards developments will further influence SAIF maturity. The EU AI Act, NIST AI Risk Management Framework, ISO/IEC 42001, ISO/IEC 23894, OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications, MITRE ATLAS, and sector-specific expectations in finance, healthcare, defence, and critical infrastructure will push organisations towards documented risk classification, explainability, testing evidence, auditability, and human oversight. Over time, enterprises are likely to move away from static governance checklists towards automated evidence collection, continuous model risk scoring, red-team automation, AI-native SOC operations, model behaviour observability, and executive dashboards that connect AI risk to business impact. Longer term, post-quantum cryptography considerations may also affect the protection of AI infrastructure, model IP, and sensitive data pipelines. SAIF’s future value will depend on its ability to remain adaptive, measurable, and operational rather than merely policy driven.

Best practices in adopting SAIF at scale

One good practice is that organisations establish clear ownership through an AI governance board or council that includes technology, cybersecurity, legal, privacy, compliance, risk, data science, finance, procurement, and business leadership. This body should define AI risk appetite, approve control baselines, classify use cases, resolve exceptions, and ensure that AI adoption remains aligned with business strategy and regulatory obligations.

A second good practice is to build a complete AI system inventory. Enterprises cannot secure what they cannot see. The inventory should include traditional ML models, genAI applications, copilots, embedded AI features, third-party AI services, open source models, vector databases, prompt libraries, agent toolchains, training datasets, feature stores, APIs, and production endpoints. Each system should be classified by risk tier based on data sensitivity, user population, autonomy level, business impact, regulatory exposure, and whether outputs influence material decisions. Low-risk internal productivity tools may follow a lighter review path, while high-impact systems in finance, healthcare, HR, legal, security, or customer decisioning should require stronger validation, audit trails, human oversight, and executive approval.

Technically, SAIF should be embedded into MLOps and LLMOps pipelines. This means using centralised model registries, version control, secure CI/CD, container scanning, signed artifacts, policy-as-code, dataset validation, model evaluation gates, prompt testing, red-team testing, and deployment approvals before production release. Retrieval-augmented systems require special attention: vector databases must enforce access control, encryption, tenant isolation, retention rules, source traceability, and protection against data leakage through embeddings or unauthorised retrieval. AI agents require scoped credentials, tool allowlists, sandboxing, rate limits, approval workflows, and continuous monitoring of tool invocation patterns.

Operationally, SAIF should connect with existing enterprise security systems. Prompt logs, model access events, anomalous outputs, suspicious tool calls, data leakage alerts, and policy violations should feed into centralised logging, SIEM, SOAR, incident response, and audit platforms. Organisations should define AI-specific incident playbooks covering prompt injection, model abuse, credential misuse, data exfiltration, poisoned datasets, unsafe outputs, and compromised agents. Vendor risk management is also essential because many AI capabilities depend on external model providers, SaaS copilots, cloud platforms, and open source components.

Scaling should occur in phases. The enterprise can begin with pilot controls for a limited number of high-value use cases, mature them into reusable guardrail services and reference architectures, and then extend them across business units. Success should be measured through control coverage, audit readiness, reduction in data leakage events, model performance stability, incident response time, policy compliance, user trust, and business adoption.

SAIF’s value is not limited to cybersecurity; it also supports responsible innovation, executive accountability, operational discipline, regulatory readiness, and long-term trust. Organisations that want to adopt AI confidently should treat SAIF as a living governance model rather than a one-time checklist. It should evolve with new use cases, emerging regulations, technology changes, security incidents, and business priorities.
