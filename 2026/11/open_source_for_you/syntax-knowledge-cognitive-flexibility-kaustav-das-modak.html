OPEN SOURCE FOR YOU · INTERVIEW · FOR U & ME · NOVEMBER 2026

“Syntax knowledge offers limited value today—cognitive flexibility matters far more”

From self-taught open source contributor to Developer Evangelist at Twilio, Kaustav Das Modak shares his perspective on the shift towards AI-driven software development, managing cognitive debt, and why open source philosophy remains the core engine of modern tech innovation.

Kaustav Das Modak, Developer Evangelist, APJ — Twilio

Interview by Niraj Sahay and Jiya Jay Singh  |  Open Source For You  |  November 2026  |  Pages 30–33

Q: You entered software engineering as a self-taught developer. Looking back, how did that path change the way you approach software systems compared to someone with a formal degree in computer science?

A: Early exposure to open source was less about the broader community and more about observing how skilled engineers built complex software. Contributing to Mozilla and Firefox OS gave me access to mentors who actively guided my learning and showed me what my next contribution could be. Naturally, being a hands-on contributor led to sharing my work. I realised I enjoyed bringing other developers into the ecosystem just by talking about what we were making. That principle has stayed with me throughout my career: start by building, gather people around a shared cause, and then build more things together.

Q: Since you have worked across the spectrum—from cloud and APIs to DevOps and static analysis at DeepSource, and now working in DevRel at Twilio—what is that one common thread in how you build and design software systems?

A: It is all about having a developer-first mindset—or what we call “wearing the customer’s shoes.” Developer tools shouldn’t try to educate or lecture engineers; they should help them get from Point A to Point B with as little friction as possible. That customer obsession is the thread that connects all my work.

Q: Having witnessed major shifts from cloud and APIs to DevOps, and now AI—do you see generative AI as just another productivity tool, or is it fundamentally changing how developers think?

A: It’s a genuine paradigm shift. AI acts as a force multiplier: it amplifies whatever a developer is already doing—the good and the bad. It certainly helps people produce more code, but more importantly, it makes software dynamic. For us at Twilio, AI isn’t just about writing software faster; it’s about liberating access and turning static user interactions into rich, real-time conversations.

Q: Do you think it is an incremental boost, or is it a total reset of the developer stack?

A: It’s neither a total reset nor just an incremental boost—it’s a massive leap forward. The real shift is where control and leverage sit. Developers can no longer skip writing specs, tests, or system designs. Because AI handles so much raw execution, thinking through system architecture ahead of time is more critical than ever.

Q: Given that you have worked primarily with languages like Go, Node.js, and Rust, how do you keep these core backend skills sharp when high-level AI tools handle so much boilerplate code today?

A: At the end of the day, accountability still sits with the engineer who submits the pull request. I use GenAI to fan out and explore multiple approaches to a problem quickly, but then I fan in and do the due diligence to actually understand the output. Whenever an LLM takes an unexpected approach, I pause, read up on the algorithm, and learn from it. Far from making developers dull, it’s helped me stay sharp in areas I might not have explored otherwise.

Q: People often confuse developer relations (DevRel) with marketing or sales. How do you define what a Developer Evangelist does, and why is this role so important for engineering platforms?

A: While Twilio sells to businesses, over 10 million developers are actually integrating our APIs. They need to know how Twilio fits into their broader stack, and that’s where DevRel comes in. We act as the connecting glue: we provide the technical depth behind marketing campaigns, run hands-on workshops to help sales convert opportunities, and bring raw feedback back to product teams. It’s an exploratory, educational role sitting right at the intersection of enterprise software and the developer community.

Q: You have advocated a lot for crafting great developer experiences. What makes a tool truly great versus just functional, and how do you spot friction in the developer workflow?

A: The ecosystem effect makes all the difference. A functional tool executes a task; a great tool is welcoming, easy to apply, and supported by a strong community, clear docs, and peripheral tooling like SDKs or MCP servers. Spotting friction requires active listening. We don’t just stand at event booths; we talk to builders about their pain points—which is how feedback led us to completely redesign the Twilio console.

Q: How do developers across the Asia-Pacific and Japan (APJ) region differ from Western developers when building communication workflows—and how do their specific needs around multi-language support and early testing shape Twilio’s regional product strategy?

A: Two main things set APJ developers apart: language diversity and a hands-on testing culture. In markets like India, Singapore, and Southeast Asia, English-only tech falls flat. Whether building public-facing AI for banks or voice agents for local consumers, developers prioritise multi-language support—in Mandarin, Hindi, Tamil, or Bahasa—from day one. Both Twilio’s communication channels and the underlying LLMs must accurately parse and respond in these languages. Additionally, APJ engineers like to build and test early. Based directly on feedback from India, Singapore, and Australia, we recently revamped our trial access programs so regional developers can get hands-on and prototype right out of the gate.

Q: How do you view the open source model of software development from a DevRel perspective?

A: I view it through a community-first lens, because a strong technical project naturally brings people together. My biggest influence here is Linus Torvalds and the Linux kernel’s approach: the technology itself births the community that builds it, not the other way around. Trying to rally a community without a solid core product usually fails. Whether it’s an open source tool or a startup chasing product-market fit, you need a clear cause, roadmap, and focused maintainers. Beyond that, open source instills incredible engineering discipline. Because all contributions are subject to public peer review, developers hold themselves to a higher standard and naturally avoid pushing sloppy code.

Q: Have you been contributing to any open source projects personally?

A: Over the last few years, my direct contributions have mostly been to my employers’ public projects. I closely follow developments in ecosystems like Node.js and Rust. While at Twilio, my contributions have been focused within our ecosystem, and prior to this, at Freshworks, I contributed significantly to the Freshworks CLI, which was a public project. The challenge is always the same: how to listen to users and prioritise what is important to the community versus what is important to the company. Commercial open source is an interesting category because you must balance responsibilities to both the community and the organisation.

Q: Has your team or Twilio as an organisation contributed to external open source projects?

A: Twilio has individuals who contribute actively. For instance, we had team members on the Node.js advisory board. Through Twilio.org, employees are encouraged to use their skills to support non-profits and community initiatives, including through technical contributions. Twilio’s open-source contributions happen largely through individuals doing their part rather than solely at an organisational level.

Q: You mentioned projects that Twilio has open sourced. Were those released recently, or have they been part of the ecosystem for a long time?

A: They have been part of the ecosystem for a long time. While Twilio’s core backend is closed, the ecosystem layer is open, and we maintain a strong presence across GitHub through organisations like Twilio Labs. Twilio Labs publishes tools like the Twilio CLI, which acts as a wrapper around the API with additional plugin capabilities. Much of our effort goes into maintaining the open source developer tooling we provide. One of my favourite projects is Dev Phone, which gives developers a virtual phone number within their laptop setup to make and test calls locally when building voice applications.

Q: How do you see AI changing the mechanics of open source collaboration—both as automated code contributors and reviewers, and as tools to help maintainers manage codebases and large pull requests?

A: AI is already heavily active at the contributor and reviewer levels, but we’re not at the point where an AI can act as a primary project maintainer. Here is how it breaks down.

Contributors: Developers have been using AI to generate and refine code before submitting pull requests for years now, and the output quality keeps improving.

Reviewers: LLMs are fantastic for exploring large, unfamiliar codebases. Instead of struggling through massive changesets, reviewers can ask an LLM to explain the overarching pull request first before diving into specific files.

Maintainers: Right now, AI’s best maintenance role is issue triaging—running initial checks on incoming bug reports and routing them to human maintainers. Relying on AI as a sole maintainer is too risky, and heavy API token usage gets expensive quickly.

Looking ahead, a promising trend is the rise of smaller, fine-tuned, task-specific language models. These run independently without high cloud token costs, making AI-assisted workflows accessible to all open source projects.

Q: Working closely with developers, how do you address the tool fatigue they face when trying out new tools constantly?

A: Tool fatigue comes directly from information overload and constantly chasing shiny new releases. Jumping to every new framework or model stops developers from building real domain depth—and sticking with a proven workflow doesn’t make your approach outdated. The underlying model or tool is just the fuel; your guardrails, fundamentals, and engineering workflows are the actual engine. If your foundations are solid, any newer model will simply execute your work better.

I take a very structured, low-stakes approach: I experiment with new agentic workflows—like fanning out sub-agents for requirements gathering using Copilot or Claude Code—on personal hobby projects. But I would never deploy an unmonitored agentic system into a high-stakes production environment. My advice is simple: stop chasing every tool. Focus on your fundamentals and guardrails first, and let the tools adapt to your workflow.

Q: Is Twilio working on its own proprietary LLM, generative AI, or agentic model?

A: We don’t build base LLMs—we build the infrastructure and data layers that power them. Others excel at training foundational models; our strength is global communications and enterprise context. With tools like our Conversations API, we take a ‘Bring Your Own LLM’ approach. We handle cross-channel messaging, real-time intelligence, and build a contextual customer memory data plane so callers don’t have to repeat themselves. While we do run smaller, specialised internal models for tasks like fraud detection, our core role is serving as the global communications network for the AI era.

Q: From Twilio’s perspective, how do open technologies and AI come together to change how developers build intelligent customer engagement experiences?

A: Writing code with an LLM is easy; deploying that AI reliably across real-time, multi-channel streams like voice, SMS, and web at scale is where the real complexity lives. While startups build intelligent AI models, they often struggle to tie them seamlessly into global communication networks with ultra-low latency. That’s where Twilio’s API infrastructure comes in. As a long-standing leader in the CPaaS space, we handle the scalability, routing, latency, and conversation state—allowing enterprises like ANA in Japan, major banks, and contact centres to run their own LLMs on top of our global communications network.

Q: What is the primary technical hurdle when moving an AI implementation from a text box demo to live production channels like voice or WhatsApp?

A: Latency is the primary concern, and it is a challenging problem to solve. You need servers positioned close to the user. If a user calls from the APJ region while servers are hosted far away, latency increases. Twilio maintains regional deployments in APJ, such as in Sydney, to minimise latency for local applications. Geographic deployment is one aspect; model optimisation is another. Selecting the right model for the task and structuring tool calls efficiently ensures that backend APIs respond quickly. When moving from demo to production, developers must assume the end user is impatient, making latency reduction a critical priority.

Q: Do engineering teams face latency issues when pushing or pulling code to central repositories?

A: No, that is generally not an issue. Distributed version control systems like Git provide everyone with a local copy of the repository. Pushing code to a host like GitHub takes only a few seconds, so developer-side latency there is negligible. Where latency actually matters is in production—when code or assets are deployed across a distributed CDN to reach the network edge.

Q: Drawing on your Site Reliability Engineering (SRE) background, how should teams handle observability when an AI agent hallucinates or fails silently instead of throwing a standard stack trace?

A: Observability shouldn’t just log failures after the fact—it needs to run as a parallel pipeline alongside live traffic to enable real-time intervention. Within Twilio’s Conversations ecosystem, we built auditability features that continuously flag when an LLM response strays from expected parameters or violates a guardrail. Developers can set up real-time webhooks to stream metrics like model confidence scores. If a confidence score dips below your set threshold, automated fallback actions—like rerouting the session to a human agent or gracefully terminating the interaction—trigger immediately.

Q: As basic code generation becomes automated, what core skills will separate top-tier engineers from average ones—and what specific attribute would you evaluate most closely if you were hiring a large engineering team today?

A: The ability to articulate a problem clearly is more important than ever. Years ago, students wrote code on paper without compilers; today, code generation is automated. Two key skills stand out. The first is code reading and comprehension. Developers must look at generated code and understand precisely what it does and whether it is correct. The second is domain expertise and system design. Moving from a junior to a senior engineer requires strong domain knowledge and fundamental engineering principles.

Core engineering fundamentals remain indispensable. Developers who use LLMs to understand code bases, write precise specifications, define goals and non-goals, and articulate requirements granularly will excel. Logical thinking, foundational computer science principles, and understanding the explicit limitations of AI models will differentiate top engineers.

Syntax and API memorisation offer limited value today—cognitive flexibility matters far more. If an engineer understands system abstraction, their team can easily pair with them to bridge technical gaps, and AI can help translate those requirements into any programming language. What separates great engineers now is clear logic, high-level abstraction, rapid iteration, and keeping feedback loops short.

Q: What mindset or approach would you recommend to developers to help them stand out in an AI-driven job market?

A: First, focus heavily on engineering fundamentals. AI tools are most effective when the developer knows what high-quality software looks like. A practical way to learn is to clone established open source repositories, use an LLM to explain the architecture, and study the implementation. Second, understand how components integrate, learn API design deeply, and set up reusable workflows and guardrails rather than repeatedly typing basic prompts. Third, engage with open source communities and practice building in public. Making personal projects public encourages accountability and better coding habits.

Q: When you speak at conferences and run workshops, what characterises your audience?

A: My audience primarily consists of developers building with Twilio, including enterprise developers, startup engineers, and software architects. They are often looking to take their implementations to the next level, such as integrating verification tools like Twilio Verify for two-factor authentication or implementing real-time voice and messaging architectures. They work across varied technical environments, ranging from legacy monoliths to microservices built on .NET, Java, Python, and JavaScript. The goal of these sessions is to help them bridge Twilio’s building blocks effectively into their specific architecture.
