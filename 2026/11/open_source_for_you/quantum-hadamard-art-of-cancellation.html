Quantum Computing: Hadamard and the Art of Cancellation

In this sixth article in the series on quantum computing, we take a close look at the Hadamard operator, learn what a basis really is, and discover that the true engine of quantum computing is not parallelism but interference.

By Dr Deepu Benson

Open Source For You  |  November 2026  |  Pages 93–98

The October 2026 OSFY installment covered Bell states, CNOT and a first look at the Hadamard gate. This sixth piece digs into the Hadamard operator, what a basis really is, quantum interference, and the no-cloning theorem—the art of cancellation rather than brute-force parallelism.

What is a basis? A little linear algebra

Giving directions across India from Nagpur illustrates the idea: any two non-parallel directions can span a plane—a basis. A qubit lives in a two-dimensional complex space. The computational (Z) basis is |0⟩ = [1 0]ᵀ and |1⟩ = [0 1]ᵀ. These vectors are orthonormal: ⟨0|1⟩ = 0 and ⟨0|0⟩ = ⟨1|1⟩ = 1. Measuring in a basis {|bᵢ⟩} yields outcome i with probability |⟨bᵢ|ψ⟩|².

The Hadamard (X) basis is |+⟩ = (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 and |−⟩ = (|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2—eigenstates of Pauli-X, sitting on the Bloch sphere’s equator while |0⟩/|1⟩ sit on the poles. Changing basis only changes the description; applying a gate changes the state. Two qubits live in four dimensions; the Bell basis {Φ±, Ψ±} is one useful orthonormal set. Microwave readout of superconducting qubits typically measures only in the computational basis.

Computational (Z): |0⟩, |1⟩  |  Hadamard (X): |+⟩, |−⟩
Orthonormality: ⟨0|1⟩=0, ⟨+|−⟩=0
Measurement: P(i) = |⟨bᵢ|ψ⟩|²

Measuring in a different basis

To measure in the X basis on hardware that only measures Z, apply H first (which rotates X onto Z), then measure. Running a Bell circuit “backward” (CNOT then H) maps Bell states back to computational states.

# qiskit13.py — prepare |+⟩/|−⟩, measure in Z or X
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit, transpile
from qiskit_aer import AerSimulator
sim = AerSimulator()
for state in ["+", "-"]:
    for basis in ["Z", "X"]:
        qc = QuantumCircuit(1, 1)
        qc.h(0)
        if state == "-":
            qc.z(0)
        if basis == "X":
            qc.h(0)
        qc.measure(0, 0)
        counts = sim.run(transpile(qc, sim), shots=1000).result().get_counts()
        print(f"|{state}> measured in {basis} basis:", counts)
# Typical: |+> in X → ~{'0': 1000}; |-> in X → ~{'1': 1000}; Z → ~50/50

Challenge: modify the script to prepare |0⟩ and |1⟩ instead of |+⟩ and |−⟩.

The Hadamard operator, up close

H = (1/√2) [[1, 1], [1, −1]]
H|0⟩=|+⟩, H|1⟩=|−⟩; H|+⟩=|0⟩, H|−⟩=|1⟩; H² = I
H = (X+Z)/√2; HXH = Z; HZH = X
H⊗n|0…0⟩ = 2−n/2 Σₓ |x⟩

H translates between Z and X bases. On the Bloch sphere it is a 180° rotation about the axis between x and z. Relative phase (the minus in |−⟩) becomes measurable after another H. Applying H to n qubits creates an equal superposition of all 2ⁿ computational states. Historical aside: Sylvester (1867) and Hadamard (1893) on Hadamard matrices.

Interference: where amplitudes cancel

Between two Hadamards a qubit carries amplitudes that can be negative and cancel—like the double-slit experiment (Young; electron versions later). The engine of quantum algorithms is interference, not “trying all answers at once.”

# qiskit14.py — H–H with optional mid-circuit peek
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit, transpile
from qiskit_aer import AerSimulator
sim = AerSimulator()
for peek in [False, True]:
    qc = QuantumCircuit(1, 2)
    qc.h(0)
    if peek:
        qc.measure(0, 0)
    qc.h(0)
    qc.measure(0, 1)
    counts = sim.run(transpile(qc, sim), shots=1000).result().get_counts()
    print("Peeking:" if peek else "No peeking:", counts)
# No peeking → {'00': 1000}; Peeking → ~equal mix of 00/01/10/11

Measuring halfway collapses the state and kills interference—analogous to checking which slit the particle took. (Qiskit prints classical bit 0 on the right.)

Myth busting: trying all answers at once

Scott Aaronson and others stress: quantum speedups come from choreographing interference so wrong amplitudes cancel and right ones reinforce—not from classical parallelism over every input.

How the hardware sees a Hadamard

IBM Heron-class native sets include X, SX, RZ and CZ. H is not native; transpilation turns it into RZ(π/2)–SX–RZ(π/2) (plus a global phase). RZ is virtual (zero duration); SX on FakeMarrakesh qubit 0 is ~36 ns with error ~1.3×10⁻⁴.

# qiskit15.py
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit, transpile
from qiskit_ibm_runtime.fake_provider import FakeMarrakesh
backend = FakeMarrakesh()
qc = QuantumCircuit(1, 1)
qc.h(0); qc.measure(0, 0)
transpiled = transpile(qc, backend, initial_layout=[0])
print(transpiled.draw())
sx = backend.target["sx"][(0,)]
print(f"{backend.name}, qubit 0: SX {sx.duration*1e9:.0f} ns, error {sx.error:.1e}")

A copying machine that refuses to copy

The no-cloning theorem (Wootters & Zurek, 1982) says no device can perfectly copy an arbitrary unknown state. CNOT copies computational-basis bits but fails on general superpositions. That constraint—and phase kickback—set up Deutsch’s algorithm and the next steps in the series.
