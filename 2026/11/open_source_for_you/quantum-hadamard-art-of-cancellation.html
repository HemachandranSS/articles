Quantum Computing: Hadamard and the Art of Cancellation

In this sixth article in the series on quantum computing, we take a close look at the Hadamard operator, learn what a basis really is, and discover that the true engine of quantum computing is not parallelism but interference.

By Dr Deepu Benson

Open Source For You  |  November 2026  |  Pages 93–98

The previous article in this series (OSFY, October 2026) ended with a puzzle and a promise. Measuring the four Bell states in the computational basis separated the Φ pair from the Ψ pair but could not tell |Φ+⟩ from |Φ−⟩ or |Ψ+⟩ from |Ψ−⟩, because within each pair the only difference was a hidden phase. Two extra gates—a CNOT followed by a Hadamard—then identified all four with certainty. The promise was to give the Hadamard operator the attention it deserves, and to examine quantum interference, the phenomenon it had quietly exploited all along. This article keeps that promise. Along the way, as in earlier articles, we shall also add a new term to our quantum computing vocabulary: the no-cloning theorem, which follows naturally from our discussion of bases.

A confession before we begin. The Hadamard gate was the first gate we met in this series and has appeared in almost every program since, yet it has had to wait until the sixth article for a close examination. The official reason for the delay is pedagogical. The unofficial reason is that every equation involving the Hadamard gate carries a 1/√2, and I have to copy and paste that square root symbol every single time. Jokes apart, the official reason is genuine. The Hadamard gate cannot be fully appreciated without the idea of a basis, a word that has appeared in this series again and again without ever being properly defined. So, being faithful to our three-level approach of code, mathematics and hardware, let us begin with a little linear algebra.

What is a basis? A little linear algebra

Imagine giving directions across India, starting from Nagpur, close to the geographical centre of the country. Any city can be located by saying how far east and how far north of Nagpur it lies. Measured on a map, Kolkata is about 960 km east and 160 km north of Nagpur, Delhi is about 190 km west and 830 km north, and Mumbai is about 650 km west and 230 km south; west and south simply count as negative amounts of east and north. Mathematicians would say that the directions east and north form a basis for the map, because they satisfy two requirements. First, every location can be reached by some combination of them; we say that they span the plane. East and west alone would not do, since no combination of them will ever take you to Delhi. Second, every location is reached by exactly one such combination; we say that they are linearly independent. Adding north-east as a third direction would spoil this, because Mumbai could then be described in endlessly many ways.

East and north are not the only directions that can do this job, either. Any two directions will serve, as long as they do not lie along the same line as east and west do. The most convenient pairs are those at right angles to each other, like east and north, and that property will matter shortly. Turn the familiar pair through 45 degrees, for example, and we get north-east and north-west. Measured along these two new directions, Delhi is reached by going about 450 km north-east and then about 720 km north-west from Nagpur. Delhi has not moved; only the language describing its position has changed. Whichever basis we choose, it always contains exactly two directions. That number is called its dimension, so our map is two-dimensional.

The state of a single qubit lives in a two-dimensional space whose coordinates are complex numbers. The vectors |0⟩ = [1 0]ᵀ and |1⟩ = [0 1]ᵀ form a basis for it, called the computational basis (also known as the standard basis or the Z basis). Writing the general qubit state as α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, as we did in the first article, simply expresses it as a combination of these two basis vectors. The computational basis is also orthonormal, and to see what that means we need the inner product. In Dirac notation, ⟨u| denotes the conjugate transpose of |u⟩: a row vector whose entries are the complex conjugates of the entries of the column vector |u⟩. The inner product ⟨u|v⟩ is the product of this row vector and the column vector |v⟩—a single number. Two vectors are orthogonal if their inner product is zero, a vector is normalised if its inner product with itself is one, and an orthonormal basis consists of mutually orthogonal, normalised vectors. It is easy to verify that ⟨0|1⟩ = ⟨1|0⟩ = 0 and ⟨0|0⟩ = ⟨1|1⟩ = 1.

With the inner product in hand, the measurement rule from the first article can be stated in its full generality: a qubit in the state |ψ⟩ measured in an orthonormal basis {|b₀⟩, |b₁⟩} yields outcome i with probability |⟨bᵢ|ψ⟩|². For the computational basis, ⟨0|ψ⟩ = α and ⟨1|ψ⟩ = β, and we recover the familiar probabilities |α|² and |β|². But the rule works for any orthonormal basis. In the fourth article we met the states |+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 and |−⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2, and called them X-basis states without explaining the word. A one-line calculation shows that ⟨+|−⟩ = (1×1 + 1×(−1))/2 = 0, and similar calculations give ⟨+|+⟩ = ⟨−|−⟩ = 1, so |+⟩ and |−⟩ form an orthonormal basis too, known as the Hadamard basis (also known as the diagonal basis or the X basis).

The letters Z and X come from the Pauli gates, which we met in the second and third articles. The Pauli-Z gate leaves |0⟩ and |1⟩ unchanged apart from a sign (Z|0⟩ = |0⟩ and Z|1⟩ = −|1⟩), and the Pauli-X gate does the same for |+⟩ and |−⟩ (X|+⟩ = |+⟩ and X|−⟩ = −|−⟩). The letters also match the Bloch sphere: the computational basis states sit at the two ends of the z-axis, the north and south poles, while the Hadamard basis states sit at the two ends of the x-axis. Note a point that confuses many beginners: orthogonal states are not at right angles on the Bloch sphere but at opposite ends of it. Figure 1 summarises both bases.

Computational (Z) basis: |0⟩ = [1 0]ᵀ, |1⟩ = [0 1]ᵀ
Hadamard (X) basis: |+⟩ = (1/√2)[1 1]ᵀ, |−⟩ = (1/√2)[1 −1]ᵀ
Orthonormality: ⟨0|1⟩ = 0, ⟨0|0⟩ = ⟨1|1⟩ = 1; ⟨+|−⟩ = 0, ⟨+|+⟩ = ⟨−|−⟩ = 1
Measurement in a basis {|b₀⟩, |b₁⟩}: P(i) = |⟨bᵢ|ψ⟩|²

Any state can be described in either basis. For example, |0⟩ = (|+⟩ + |−⟩)/√2 is the same state written in the language of the Hadamard basis. Rewriting a state in another basis changes only its description; applying a gate changes the state itself. All of this extends naturally to more qubits. Two qubits live in a four-dimensional space, and the computational basis {|00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩} is the obvious basis for it. But in the previous article we met a second orthonormal basis for exactly the same space: the Bell basis {|Φ+⟩, |Φ−⟩, |Ψ+⟩, |Ψ−⟩}. Every vector in the computational basis is a product state, while every vector in the Bell basis is maximally entangled. In general, n qubits live in a space of dimension 2ⁿ, which is why fifty qubits already need 2⁵⁰ amplitudes—more than a quadrillion (10¹⁵).

A fact from the hardware level ties all this together. The microwave readout of a superconducting qubit, described in the previous article, can answer exactly one question: is the qubit in |0⟩ or in |1⟩? In other words, the hardware measures only in the computational basis. To measure in any other basis, we first apply a gate that rotates that basis onto the computational basis and then measure. This is exactly what happened at the end of the previous article. The circuit that prepares the Bell states—a Hadamard gate on qubit 0 followed by a CNOT with qubit 0 as control and qubit 1 as target—carries the four computational basis states onto the four Bell states. Run backwards, CNOT first and then the Hadamard gate, it carries each Bell state back to a distinct computational basis state that the hardware can read: |Φ+⟩ to 00, |Φ−⟩ to 01, |Ψ+⟩ to 10 and |Ψ−⟩ to 11, with Qiskit printing qubit 1 on the left. A Bell measurement is therefore nothing more mysterious than a change of basis followed by an ordinary measurement. For a single qubit, the gate that rotates the Hadamard basis onto the computational basis is the Hadamard gate itself, which is why the basis bears its name: H turns |+⟩ into |0⟩ and |−⟩ into |1⟩.

Measuring in a different basis

The program qiskit13.py, shown below, prepares the states |+⟩ and |−⟩, and measures each of them twice: once in the computational basis (the Z basis) and once in the Hadamard basis (the X basis).

# qiskit13.py
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit, transpile
from qiskit_aer import AerSimulator
sim = AerSimulator()
for state in ["+", "-"]:
    for basis in ["Z", "X"]:
        qc = QuantumCircuit(1, 1)
        qc.h(0)
        if state == "-":
            qc.z(0)
        if basis == "X":
            qc.h(0)
        qc.measure(0, 0)
        compiled = transpile(qc, sim)
        counts = sim.run(compiled, shots=1000).result().get_counts()
        print(f"|{state}> measured in {basis} basis:", counts)

Now let us understand how the program works. Lines 4 and 5 loop over the states and bases. Line 7 prepares |+⟩ with a Hadamard gate, and Lines 8 and 9 turn it into |−⟩ with a Pauli-Z gate when required—recall from the third article that Z leaves |0⟩ alone and multiplies |1⟩ by −1. Lines 10 and 11 contain the only new idea: when we want to measure in the X basis, we apply one more Hadamard gate before the measurement. Lines 12 to 15 measure, run 1000 shots and print the counts. Activate qiskit_env and run the program with the command python qiskit13.py. The output is shown in Figure 2. Recall that the counts obtained on your terminal will differ slightly from run to run.

Measured in the Z basis, |+⟩ and |−⟩ behave identically, each producing roughly 500 zeros and 500 ones. This is the same disappointment we met with |Φ+⟩ and |Φ−⟩ in the previous article. Measured in the X basis, however, |+⟩ yields 0 in all 1000 shots, and |−⟩ yields 1 in all 1000 shots. Orthogonal states can always be told apart by a suitable measurement, but not by every measurement. Randomness is therefore not a property of a quantum state alone; it is a property of a state together with the question we ask it. Try preparing |0⟩ and measuring it in the X basis, and you will find that the roles are reversed: the state that is certain in the Z basis behaves like a fair coin in the X basis. Take it as a challenge: to make qiskit13.py prepare |0⟩ and |1⟩ instead of |+⟩ and |−⟩, make cosmetic changes to the state labels in Lines 4 and 8, replace the Z gate in Line 9 with the gate that turns |0⟩ into |1⟩ (Hint: read the second article in this series), and then remove Line 7.

The Hadamard operator, up close

In the first article, we met the Hadamard gate as a 2×2 matrix: 1/√2 times the matrix with rows (1, 1) and (1, −1), shown again in Figure 3. Look at its columns. The first column is |+⟩ and the second is |−⟩, so H sends |0⟩ to |+⟩ and |1⟩ to |−⟩. Moreover, multiplying H by itself gives the identity matrix, H² = I, so the Hadamard gate is its own inverse and sends |+⟩ back to |0⟩ and |−⟩ back to |1⟩. The Hadamard gate is therefore far more than a quantum coin-flipper: it is a translator between two languages, converting the computational basis into the Hadamard basis and back again. This is exactly why it appears in Lines 10 and 11 of qiskit13.py. Notice, too, the lonely minus sign in the bottom-right corner. Without it, the two columns would be identical rather than orthogonal, and the matrix would not be a valid quantum gate; that minus sign is what allows amplitudes to cancel.

Figure 3 also shows a few more identities. The Hadamard matrix can be written as H = (X + Z)/√2, a perfectly balanced blend of two Pauli gates. More importantly, HXH = Z and HZH = X. In words, a bit flip viewed through Hadamard glasses becomes a phase flip, and a phase flip becomes a bit flip. This explains the experiment of the third article, in which the sequence Hadamard, Pauli-Z, Hadamard took the qubit from |0⟩ to |1⟩ with certainty: the three gates together are simply an X gate in disguise. On the Bloch sphere, the Hadamard gate is a 180° rotation about the diagonal axis halfway between the x-axis and the z-axis. That rotation swaps the poles with the equator and turns relative phase into something a Z-basis measurement can see.

H = (1/√2) [[1, 1], [1, −1]]
H|0⟩ = |+⟩, H|1⟩ = |−⟩; H|+⟩ = |0⟩, H|−⟩ = |1⟩; H² = I
H = (X + Z)/√2; HXH = Z; HZH = X
H⊗n|0…0⟩ = 2−n/2 Σx∈{0,1}n |x⟩

The real power of the Hadamard gate appears when it is applied to many qubits at once. Applying H to each of n qubits starting in |0…0⟩ produces an equal superposition of all 2ⁿ computational basis states, each with amplitude 1/√(2ⁿ). That opening move is the start of many well-known algorithms—and the source of a persistent myth, to which we shall return shortly. Historically, matrices with this ±1 pattern were studied by James Joseph Sylvester in 1867; they are now called Hadamard matrices after Jacques Hadamard, who studied them in 1893, and the gate was named after them.

Interference: Where amplitudes cancel

In the first decade of the 1800s the English polymath Thomas Young showed that light passing through two narrow slits produces, on a screen behind them, a pattern of bright and dark bands. At the dark bands, light from one slit cancels light from the other: light added to light produces darkness. In 1961, Claus Jönsson repeated the experiment with electrons, and later versions sent the electrons through one at a time, one by one; the same interference built up. Richard Feynman, whom we quoted in the first article, wrote that this double-slit experiment contains the only mystery of quantum mechanics.

Something similar happens when two Hadamard gates act in succession. The first H turns |0⟩ into (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2. The second H acts on each part: the |0⟩ component becomes (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/2 and the |1⟩ component becomes (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/2 (after the extra 1/√2). The two routes to outcome 0 add constructively (+1/2 and +1/2 = 1), while the two routes to outcome 1 cancel (+1/2 and −1/2 = 0). Amplitudes can be negative, and negatives cancel positives. A colourful classical analogy appears in a MythBusters episode in which a soccer ball fired backwards at 60 mph from a truck moving forward at 60 mph ends up nearly still relative to the ground—velocities cancel. Quantum amplitudes cancel in much the same spirit, except that the “velocities” are complex numbers attached to paths through a circuit.

The engine of quantum algorithms is therefore interference, not “trying all answers at once.” Interference is fragile, as the program qiskit14.py, shown below, reveals. It runs the H–H circuit twice: once undisturbed, and once with a sneaky measurement between the two gates.

# qiskit14.py
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit, transpile
from qiskit_aer import AerSimulator
sim = AerSimulator()
for peek in [False, True]:
    qc = QuantumCircuit(1, 2)
    qc.h(0)
    if peek:
        qc.measure(0, 0)
    qc.h(0)
    qc.measure(0, 1)
    compiled = transpile(qc, sim)
    counts = sim.run(compiled, shots=1000).result().get_counts()
    print("Peeking:" if peek else "No peeking:", counts)

Now let us understand how the program works. Line 4 runs the experiment twice: first without the peek (peek is False) and then with it (peek is True). Line 5 creates one qubit and two classical bits. Lines 6 and 9 are the two Hadamard gates. Lines 7 and 8 record a mid-circuit measurement in classical bit 0, but only when peek is True. Line 10 records the final measurement in classical bit 1. Lines 11 to 13 run 1000 shots and print the counts, just as in qiskit13.py. Remember the bit-ordering convention from the previous article: Qiskit prints classical bit 0 on the right, so in each result the right-hand character is the peek and the left-hand character is the final outcome. Run the program with the command python qiskit14.py. The output is shown in Figure 4.

Without peeking, every shot gives '00': the final outcome is 0, exactly as predicted, and the right-hand 0 is simply the unused classical bit. With peeking, all four two-bit results appear roughly 250 times each, because the peek is random and so is the final outcome. Merely looking at the qubit halfway through has destroyed the interference: after the mid-circuit measurement, the qubit is definitely in |0⟩ or in |1⟩; only one route remains, and there is nothing for it to cancel against. Physicists say that the measurement has made which-path information available, which destroys interference; the double-slit experiment in physics behaves likewise when detectors watch the slits.

On a real processor, interference can be lost even when nobody peeks. Some of the hardware errors we met in the previous article, such as dephasing, scramble the relative phase on which interference depends, and the qubit then behaves just like the one we peeked at: probabilities add, and nothing cancels. It is as if the environment was peeking at our qubits, although that is only an analogy.

Myth busting: Trying all answers at once

Popular accounts often claim that a quantum computer is fast because, after H⊗n, its register holds all 2ⁿ inputs at once and evaluates a function on all of them in parallel. The first half is true; the conclusion is not. Measuring such a register yields a single random basis state, no better than a guess: the 2ⁿ amplitudes genuinely exist, but we can never read them all out. A quantum algorithm succeeds only by choreographing interference, so that the amplitudes of wrong answers cancel and those of right answers reinforce one another before the measurement. Even that is no magic wand: it works only when a problem has structure that a clever circuit can exploit, which is why quantum computers are expected to speed up certain problems dramatically, others modestly, and many not at all. The computer scientist Scott Aaronson has long carried this disclaimer at the top of his blog: “If you take nothing else from this blog: quantum computers won’t solve hard problems instantly by just trying all solutions in parallel.” Parallelism sets the stage; interference performs the play.

How the hardware sees a Hadamard gate

In the previous article we learned that IBM’s Heron processors support only a short list of native gates: X, SX, RZ and CZ. So how does the chip perform a Hadamard gate? The qiskit-ibm-runtime package, which we installed in the fourth article, includes fake backends: offline models of real IBM processors, stored with their native gates, qubit connectivity and a snapshot of their calibration data. The program qiskit15.py, shown below, transpiles a single Hadamard gate for FakeMarrakesh, a model of ibm_marrakesh, the 156-qubit Heron R2 processor we used in the fourth article. Nothing is sent to IBM and no API key is needed: the program only translates the circuit; it does not run it. For once, we skip our small tradition of sending code to a real quantum computer; to see how a gate is translated, a model of the processor is all we need.

# qiskit15.py
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit, transpile
from qiskit_ibm_runtime.fake_provider import FakeMarrakesh
backend = FakeMarrakesh()
qc = QuantumCircuit(1, 1)
qc.h(0)
qc.measure(0, 0)
transpiled = transpile(qc, backend, initial_layout=[0])
print(transpiled.draw())
sx = backend.target["sx"][(0,)]
print(f"{backend.name}, qubit 0: SX {sx.duration*1e9:.0f} ns, error {sx.error:.1e}")

Line 7 places our qubit on physical qubit 0 and rewrites the circuit using native gates. The output, shown in Figure 5, reveals that the Hadamard gate becomes RZ(π/2), then SX, then RZ(π/2) again, together with a global phase of π/4, which, as we have seen, changes no measurement probability. Better still, RZ gates on IBM hardware are virtual: instead of sending a pulse to the qubit, the control system simply shifts the phase reference of every subsequent pulse in software. The calibration snapshot in the version of qiskit-ibm-runtime used for this article (0.48.0) lists RZ with zero duration and zero error, while SX on qubit 0 takes 36 nanoseconds with an error rate of about 1.3 in 10,000. Lines 9 and 10 print the stored SX values, as the last line of Figure 5 shows; another version of the package may store different ones. So, on this processor model, the Hadamard gate costs a single physical microwave pulse, the SX gate, sandwiched between two virtual RZ gates that exist only as bookkeeping in the control software. Add now two more lines, qc.z(0) and qc.h(0), immediately after qc.h(0) in Line 5, and run the program again: the transpiler reduces all three gates to a single X gate—the identity HZH = X at work.

A copying machine that refuses to copy

We end with a short aside on whether a qubit can be copied, a question that the discussion of bases raises naturally. Copying the bits of a classical register is easy, but what about the states of a quantum register? With a spare qubit in |0⟩, a CNOT gate turns |0⟩|0⟩ into |0⟩|0⟩ and |1⟩|0⟩ into |1⟩|1⟩: it copies answers in the computational basis from the control qubit onto the target. Have we built a quantum photocopier? Let’s feed it |+⟩. A true copier would produce |+⟩|+⟩ = (|00⟩ + |01⟩ + |10⟩ + |11⟩)/2. Instead, as we have seen ever since the fourth article, the result is (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2—the Bell state |Φ+⟩. We asked for a copy and received entanglement. Quantum gates are linear, so any gate that copies |0⟩ and |1⟩ in this way cannot copy their superposition. A similar argument shows that a single device can copy two different states only if they are orthogonal, and even then it fails on general superpositions. This is the no-cloning theorem, published in 1982 by William Wootters and Wojciech Zurek, and independently by Dennis Dieks: no quantum operation can make a perfect copy of an arbitrary unknown state. It does not forbid copying the states of one known orthonormal basis, as the CNOT gate does, nor preparing a known state twice by running its preparation circuit again.

It is now time to conclude. We defined a basis, learned that the hardware measures only in the computational basis, and met the Hadamard gate as the translator that lets us measure in the X basis. We watched amplitudes cancel, saw how a mid-circuit measurement spoils that cancellation, buried the myth of trying every answer at once, and followed a Hadamard gate as it was translated into the native gates of a processor model. Finally, a CNOT gate taught us that an unknown qubit cannot be cloned. In the next article we shall put interference to work on our first quantum algorithm, Deutsch’s: asking whether a function of one bit is constant or balanced—in a single query to the function, whereas any classical method needs two queries to be sure. Along the way, we shall meet phase kickback, a trick that hides the answer in a phase until a final Hadamard gate brings it into the open.
