Quantum Computing Unlocked

Quantum computing is poised to revolutionise industries by finding solutions to challenges once deemed intractable.

By: Dr Gopala Krishna Behara and Narumanchi Sree Keerthi

Open Source For You  |  November 2026  |  Pages 40–44

Quantum computing has moved beyond academic exploration into early commercial deployment, with active pilots across finance, healthcare, logistics, and cybersecurity. Enterprises are running proof-of-concepts for portfolio optimisation, molecular simulation, and quantum-safe encryption to prepare for the arrival of a cryptographically relevant quantum computer.

According to McKinsey’s 2026 Quantum Technology Monitor, more than 300 global companies including Airbus, JPMorgan Chase, Boehringer Ingelheim, and Liberty Mutual are now actively collaborating with quantum technology vendors, and the technology could unlock as much as US$ 2.7 trillion in economic value across industries by 2035.

Classical computing vs quantum computing

Conventional silicon-based computers consist of millions of transistors that switch between ‘on’ and ‘off’, corresponding to the two possible values of bits — 0 and 1. Processing relies on basic logic gates AND, NOT, and the like, acting on individual bits or pairs of bits. Each bit’s state can be measured and distinguished without disturbing the system.

Quantum computers, by contrast, employ quantum bits or qubits that can occupy a superposition of both 0 and 1 at the same time. A two-qubit device can represent four states simultaneously, a three-qubit system eight states, a four-qubit system sixteen states, and an n-qubit can register up to 2n unique states in parallel. Qubits can also become entangled, which means the state of one qubit is intrinsically linked to the state of another — a defining feature of quantum mechanics. This inherent parallelism allows quantum machines to tackle specific classes of complex problems that are far beyond the reach of classical architectures.

Characteristics of a quantum computing platform (QCP)

  • Low level programming: Quantum computers are built on low level programming based on quantum logical gates to handle computational steps that execute in quantum processing units (QPUs). Examples of quantum logical gates are Pauli, Hadamard, CCNot, etc.
  • Heterogeneous: Technical specifications of the various quantum computing platforms (QCPs) are heterogeneous with respect to both the software and hardware in QPUs.
  • Remote software development and deployment: QCP vendors provide the quantum computing software development framework for leveraging quantum processors that are accessed remotely on the cloud. Only a limited portion of the programming tool stack is deployed on local machines. Programmers access production-ready quantum software remotely for development and testing.

  • IBM, Google, Microsoft, Quantinuum, and IonQ have all hit logical-qubit milestones. Highlights include Microsoft/Quantinuum’s 12 logical qubits (March 2026), IBM’s 4,158-qubit Kookaburra system, and Google’s proof of exponential error suppression via surface-code scaling.
  • According to the Grand View Research report on quantum computing, the US government acquired equity worth US$ 2 billion in nine quantum firms. D-Wave Systems acquired Quantum Computing Inc. for US$ 550 million and Google/Nvidia backed QuEra’s US$ 230+ million funding round.
  • A Roots Analysis report says that Quantum Computing-as-a-Service (cloud-based access) captured approximately 40% share in 2026, since it removes the need for costly hardware investment.
  • A BQPSim report says that, in 2026, AWS Braket, Google Quantum AI, IBM Quantum Network, and Microsoft Azure Quantum all expanded pay-as-you-go access to quantum processing units.
  • According to Stellar Market Research, healthcare and pharmaceuticals are projected to see the highest CAGR (around 29%) in quantum computing among end-use segments through the mid-2030s. Growth will be driven by drug discovery, genome sequencing, and molecular simulation.

Quantum algorithms: Programmers leverage quantum algorithms to define the computing task. Algorithms help in gaining speed and communicating with other computing tasks that are running on QCPs. Programmers must also identify or design suitable quantum algorithms that can solve the problem on hand.

Portability of software: Quantum computing platforms are currently evolving and the software developed by the platform owners are native in nature. This software follows their own standards, with proprietary programming APIs and tools.

QCP architecture

QCP architecture consists of an end-to-end quantum computing system with seven layers, starting with the physical qubit at the bottom and progressing upwards to the application/business layer (Figure 1). Quantum computation is a tightly coupled system of quantum hardware, cryogenic infrastructure, control electronics, real-time software, compilers, orchestration, and classical computing.

QPU and physical qubits: The quantum execution layer consists of quantum information physically existing in quantum gates, interactions and measurements.

Cryogenic environment and interconnect: This layer provides the physical environment and signal path between room-temperature electronics and the QPU. It is essentially the infrastructure that allows the QPU to live at millikelvin temperatures while still being controlled from the classical environment.

Control and readout electronics: This layer converts digital instructions into physical electrical/microwave signals that manipulate the qubits. It also performs the reverse operation: converting very weak signals coming from the QPU into usable digital data.

Quantum classical interface/ control highway: This acts as a quantum computer’s high-speed, thermally managed nervous system. It is the boundary and transport mechanism between classical electronics and the quantum device.

Classical control and quantum orchestration: This layer is the real-time brain coordinating the quantum machine. It sits between the quantum software stack and the physical control system.

Quantum software, compiler and runtime: This layer translates human/programmer intent into executable quantum operations.

Application and business layer: This is the layer where business value is created. It leverages quantum software applications that help in meeting the business requirements of an enterprise. Main application areas are:

  • Optimisation: Supply-chain optimisation, scheduling, portfolio optimisation, route optimisation
  • Chemistry: Molecular simulation, drug discovery, catalyst design
  • Materials: Materials simulation, battery materials, superconducting materials
  • Finance: Portfolio optimisation, risk analysis, pricing
  • Cybersecurity: Cryptographic research, security analysis
  • Scientific computing: Physics simulation, quantum chemistry, materials science

Algorithms in quantum computing

A quantum algorithm is a step-by-step procedure, expressed as a quantum circuit, that exploits superposition, entanglement, and interference to solve a problem with fewer operations than the best-known classical algorithm. Table 2 summarises the algorithms that matter most in 2026.

Table 2: Leading quantum algorithms

Algorithm What it does Speedup / type Typical use case
Shor’s algorithm Factors large integers and solves discrete logarithms in polynomial time Exponential (over best-known classical) Breaking RSA/ECC encryption: the primary driver of post-quantum cryptography migration
Grover’s algorithm Searches an unsorted database of N items Quadratic (√N vs N classically) Unstructured search, database lookup, brute-force cryptanalysis of symmetric keys
Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT) Quantum analogue of the discrete Fourier transform, converted into a sequence of quantum gates Exponential over classical FFT for certain uses Core subroutine inside Shor’s algorithm and quantum phase estimation
Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) Hybrid quantum-classical algorithm that estimates the lowest eigenvalue (ground-state energy) of a molecule or material Problem-dependent; near-term/NISQ-friendly Molecular simulation, drug discovery, catalyst and battery-material design
Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) Hybrid algorithm that approximates optimal solutions to combinatorial optimisation problems Heuristic speedup on specific problem structures Portfolio optimisation, route planning, scheduling, max-cut style problems
HHL (Harrow-Hassidim-Lloyd) algorithm Solves systems of linear equations by encoding the solution in quantum amplitudes Exponential under specific sparsity/conditioning assumptions Foundational subroutine for quantum machine learning and quantum-enhanced linear algebra
Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE) Estimates the eigenvalue (phase) associated with a unitary operator’s eigenvector Exponential precision scaling Building block for Shor’s algorithm, VQE refinement, and chemistry simulation
Quantum Annealing Uses adiabatic evolution to settle a system into its lowest-energy configuration Heuristic; no proven general speedup Combinatorial optimisation on annealing hardware (D-Wave)
Quantum Machine Learning (QSVM, QNN) Quantum-native variants of support vector machines and neural networks using quantum feature spaces Problem-dependent; theoretical speedups for specific data structures Pattern classification, anomaly detection, high-dimensional data analysis

The current quantum landscape

Various tech leaders are working on quantum computing at present. IBM, Microsoft, Alibaba, D-Wave Systems, Google, Nokia, Fujitsu, and Intel continue to compete for authority in this field.

IBM: IBM has been working to develop a quantum computer for over 35 years. In December 2023, it unveiled a 1,121-qubit processor called Condor, setting a new industry benchmark for qubit count. IBM Quantum Experience is a cloud platform where experiments can be carried out on quantum hardware. Multinational companies such as JPMorgan Chase, Daimler (logistics for optimising vehicle delivery routes), Honda, Samsung and Barclays (settlement of large batches of transactions) were the first to sign up for the active testing of this quantum cloud service.

Google: Google’s most recent quantum processor is called Willow. Unveiled in December 2024, Willow is a 105-qubit superconducting chip that for the first time demonstrates exponential error suppression as qubit count scales. Google is aiming for a useful, error-corrected quantum computer by 2029, which is ambitious.

Microsoft: Microsoft has announced Majorana 1, the first QPU to be powered by topological qubits. Azure Quantum provides access to various quantum hardware and quantum-inspired services. Microsoft is also collaborating with other quantum hardware providers to broaden quantum access and accelerate development.

D-Wave Systems: D-Wave built the world’s first commercially available quantum computer with a simpler technology but limited in capability. The company is focusing on how quantum computing can be applied to artificial intelligence, machine learning, and difficult optimisation problems. In 2025, it developed a 4400+ qubit called D-Wave Advantage2 to address cybersecurity and logistics optimisation.

Rigetti Computing: Rigetti recently launched a 36-qubit multi-chip processor, which provides a full-stack solution that tightly integrates hardware and software, offering low-latency cloud access for hybrid quantum-classical workflows.

Intel: Intel has announced the delivery of a 49-qubit test quantum processor called ‘Tangle Lake’, and ‘Tunnel Falls’, a 12-qubit silicon chip.

Amazon Web Services: AWS introduced its Ocelot chip in February 2025, built around ‘cat qubits’ designed to reduce error-correction overhead. It continues to operate Braket, a multi-vendor cloud quantum computing service spanning IonQ, Rigetti, D-Wave, and Oxford Quantum Circuits hardware.

Nvidia: In March 2025, Nvidia launched the Nvidia Accelerated Quantum Research Center (NVAQC) in Boston. This centre is all about developing new quantum computing architectures and algorithms that can work together with their powerful AI supercomputers.

IonQ: IonQ has developed quantum computers based on trapped ion technology.

Alibaba: Alibaba has designed an experimental quantum computer which is available online. In March 2018, it had launched its ‘superconducting quantum computing cloud’ to provide access to an 11-qubit quantum computer. Developed along with the Chinese Academy of Sciences, it allows users to run quantum programs and download the results.

Quantinuum: Formed from the merger of Cambridge Quantum and Honeywell Quantum Solutions, Quantinuum builds high-fidelity trapped-ion systems (the H-series and Helios). It filed for a US IPO in January 2026, alongside Microsoft, on the strength of its logical-qubit demonstrations.

QuEra Computing: Spun out of Harvard and MIT research, QuEra uses neutral atoms held in optical tweezer arrays as qubits, and has demonstrated some of the highest logical qubit counts in the industry on its Aquila hardware.

Other leading quantum computing vendors are Xanadu, Pasqal, Riverlane, Honeywell International, and Huawei Technologies Co Ltd.

The implications of true quantum computing at scale are staggering and of extraordinary impact to society. Quantum algorithms are applied for:

  • Optimisation problems such as scheduling and route planning to find out the best possible decision out of the large number of decisions or options that are available
  • Search, sampling and pattern matching
  • Quantum encryption

The challenges

Lack of proven software advantage: Only a handful of quantum algorithms (VQE, QAOA, Grover, Shor) demonstrate meaningful, proven speedups today; most real-world problems still lack demonstrated quantum advantage on hardware.

Hybrid orchestration complexity: Integrating quantum subroutines into classical workflows requires robust middleware, cross-stack compatibility, and new programming paradigms.

Technological/error-correction overhead: Even with 2025–2026’s error-correction breakthroughs, encoding one logical qubit still requires dozens to hundreds of physical qubits, depending on the code family. Detecting, controlling, and correcting errors in real time remains a major engineering challenge.

Benchmarking and standardisation gap: The absence of industrywide metrics and transparent performance benchmarks hampers an objective comparison of hardware and software stacks.

Vendor lock-in: Proprietary SDKs and cloud APIs lack unified standards, complicated multi-platform development and collaboration.

Energy and infrastructure costs at scale: Quantum computing at scale requires massive physical infrastructure like cryogenics, control electronics, specialised materials, and high-density wiring that drive energy consumption to levels comparable to or exceeding modern data centres. ‘Quantum’ does not inherently mean ‘low-energy’, and scaling towards fault-tolerance introduces steep thermodynamic and engineering overheads.

Paucity of talent and academia-industry collaboration: Demand for quantum-literate engineers, physicists, and algorithm designers continues to outstrip supply, and closer collaboration between industry and academia remains a persistent need.

Cryogenic infrastructure challenges: Quantum computers operate at temperatures close to absolute zero, colder than the vacuum of space. Maintaining such a low temperature is a big challenge.

Accelerating post-quantum cryptography migration: The global shift to post-quantum cryptography (PQC) is now time-bound. With formal PQC standards finalised and government compliance deadlines converging in the early 2030s, enterprises face a shrinking window to inventory their cryptographic assets, modernise protocols, and mitigate ‘harvest-now, decrypt-later’ risks before they translate into material breaches.

Modern enterprises are actively exploring cloud-based quantum computing to enhance productivity and operational efficiency, anticipating that quantum computing, combined with advanced AI, machine learning, and business analytics, will deliver greater agility in their business processes. Enterprises are also increasingly exploring quantum hardware roadmaps, error-mitigation techniques, and hybrid quantum-classical algorithm frameworks that bridge classical systems with emerging quantum processors, while, in parallel, urgently building post-quantum cryptography readiness ahead of the eventual arrival of a cryptographically relevant quantum computer.
