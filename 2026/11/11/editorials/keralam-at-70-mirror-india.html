KERALA · HUMAN DEVELOPMENT · FEDERALISM

Keralam at 70: holding a mirror to India

Nissim Mannathukkaren

Dalhousie University, Canada.

Op-Ed  |  November 11, 2026

In 1956, Keralam was amongst the poorest States, on par with Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh. Presently, it is amongst the richest; but more importantly, it has a lower infant mortality than the U.S. If Keralam were counted as a nation, it would rank 75 on the Human Development Index, ahead of China. It would also be 55 ranks above India which is at 130. This transformation and divergence from the national path is the globally lauded ‘Keralam story’. This story, which is, of course, not seamless and unblemished, is built on a central animator: a robust democracy, which is a rarity in the Global South. Its significance is enhanced further because Keralam is also among the most religiously diverse regions.

Keralam reinforces Amartya Sen’s original thesis that development is about expanding human freedoms rather than mere economic growth; human development does not require high incomes. Yet even now the central thrust of the national development regime is “Viksit Bharat 2047,” an income-based development measure. If India had the same infant mortality levels as that of Keralam, it would have saved over 5,00,000 infant lives annually.

This staggering reality is ignored in privileging economic growth over distribution, wellbeing and democracy. Take Gujarat, a State which is richer and growing faster than Keralam and projected as a model of economic development. Keralam’s multi-dimensional poverty rates are 20 times less, and infant mortality levels four times less than Gujarat. Further, 87% women have at least 10 years of schooling in Keralam while in Gujarat, it is 38%. Similarly, there are 82% men in Keralam with 10 years of schooling compared to Gujarat’s 47%. Only 2.9% of women aged 20-24 years were married before 18 in Keralam, while it is 18% in Gujarat. 10.9% of children under five are wasted in Keralam while in Gujarat it is 20.2%; rural open defecation practices in Keralam are at 0%; in Gujarat, 32%. The excess deaths in Keralam were 1.5 times the official COVID-19 estimates; in Gujarat it was 33 times more.

Besides, human development is not in a zero-sum process with economic growth. As economist K.P. Kannan shows, demographic transition added 1% in per capita growth per annum compared to national growth, and migration fuelled economic growth.

Model for all

At a time when majoritarian nationalism encompasses large parts of India, Keralam at 70 is still offering a vision of secularism and pluralism. It is built on coexistence consisting of the oldest Christian and Muslim communities of India, and millennia of global exchange and cosmopolitanism. This has led to consociational power-sharing among religious communities ensuring near proportional representation in legislatures and cabinets. The current Keralam Assembly has 51% Hindu, 25% Muslim, and 24% Christian MLAs roughly corresponding to their populations. Such consociationalism is rare, even globally.

“At a time when majoritarian nationalism encompasses large parts of India, Keralam at 70 is still offering a vision of secularism and pluralism.”

Keralam has gone the farthest among States in empowering Muslims as equal citizens, thus seeking to fulfil the Constitution’s original vision. Along with religious conviviality, robust democracies also need social capital and deep civil society activism. For instance, Kudumbashree is among the world’s largest women self-help networks, and around 60% of the nation’s palliative care services are in Keralam. The defining feature of Keralam’s civil society is the critical mass of independent organisations and voices that have challenged and pushed the State and all political parties in more democratic directions.

But Keralam’s alterity, in terms of religion, food habits, culture, politics, and development evokes angst in the Hindu nationalist imagination. Thus, Keralam at 70 is facing an unprecedented challenge: relentless hostility from the national ideological consensus. Crucially, the hostility is not just cultural, but also political and economic, as federalism is being weakened.

Keralam, nevertheless, is not a finished “model”. It is an incomplete one in which there is relentless struggle to secure the promise of equality and freedoms by those who are not yet fully at the table which includes women, especially, labouring lower class/caste women, Dalits, Adivasis, migrant workers, etc. They embody the attitude theorised by philosopher Michel Foucault, of critically interrogating the present to expose its silences.

Keralam’s past laurels will not solve other present challenges: the ecological crisis, an ageing population, fiscal pressures, migration-dependence and enveloping neoliberalism. Even so, Keralam at 70 still has a mirror to show India at a critical juncture of the latter’s own history.
