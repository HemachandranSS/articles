Strategic autonomy must guide India’s Starlink policy

Ashwin Prasad Rao

Staff Research Analyst, Advanced Military Technologies and Outer Space Programme, Takshashila Institution.

Op-Ed  |  November 11, 2026

Recently, Elon Musk unleashed a volley of media posts, accusing Indian “oligarchs” of impeding Starlink’s entry into India. The government rejected the accusation and clarified that all satellite operators must go through the same regulatory process. The ensuing public debate has centred on Mr. Musk, Mukesh Ambani, protectionism and telecom competition. All this obscures important questions of how much control India will have over Starlink once it operates here.

In its current form, Starlink is unlikely to replace fibre and mobile networks because they have almost opposite strengths. Fibre and mobile networks take time and money to set up, but can serve many people in one area. Starlink reaches remote places without towers or cables but is limited by how many users it can serve in one region. While fibre and mobile networks are optimised for density, Starlink is optimised for reach. Starlink has previously stopped taking new residential customers in areas where it ran out of capacity.

Starlink is well-suited for isolated settlements, border regions, ships, and disaster-affected areas. Governments and the military require reliable connectivity in such areas. Starlink can connect soldiers, drones, sensors and command centres without traditional connectivity. Therefore, its operation in India goes beyond retail customer choices and has national security implications.

The risks for India

India has very limited control over Starlink. The network could remain usable in an area where Indian authorities have ordered it switched off. Conversely, it could be disabled when India needs it most. India can licence and penalise Starlink, but SpaceX retains direct control over its satellites, software and terminal access.

The government has recognised these risks and imposed stringent security requirements on satellite Internet providers. Operators must route Indian user traffic through ground stations, called gateways, located in India. They cannot use inter-satellite links to bypass Indian gateways or route Indian traffic through gateways abroad. They must also provide monitoring facilities to Indian security agencies and restrict connectivity when authorities issue security-related orders.

“India must balance Starlink’s benefits with national security and strategic autonomy.”

Starlink has indicated willingness to comply. It announced Indian gateway sites and introduced India-specific data controls. Some of these conditions are difficult to enforce independently. Indian authorities can inspect domestic gateways, but verifying whether Indian traffic has been routed through foreign gateways or mirrored abroad through satellite links is more difficult and depends partly on information provided by SpaceX.

There is also a trade-off. Starlink’s advantage is that its satellites can pass traffic between themselves when a ground station is unavailable. If Indian gateways fail, however, these restrictions prevent Starlink from rerouting Indian traffic through gateways abroad, reducing one source of redundancy.

Global experiences

Iran’s experience is also a valuable demonstration of the enforcement problem. Since 2023, Iran has complained to the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) about Starlink terminals illegally operating on its soil without authorisation. The ITU’s Radio Regulations Board has repeatedly called for Starlink transmissions to be stopped over Iran. At its October 2026 meeting, the Board again noted that unauthorised Starlink transmissions in Iran were continuing. It reiterated its earlier decisions calling for the transmissions to stop and its conclusion that it lacked the authority to suspend the relevant satellite frequency assignments.

Ukraine provides an interesting contrast. In February, Ukrainian authorities worked with SpaceX to identify authorised Starlink terminals and block those used by Russian forces. In Ukraine, SpaceX acted on the government’s requests. In Iran, years of appeals have failed to achieve the same result.

Conversely, on October 14, Ukrainian officials reported that Starshield, the military service built on Starlink technology, began losing connectivity on terminals travelling faster than about 160 km/h, disrupting drone missions and frontline logistics. Zelenskyy said the restrictions applied in Ukraine but not in EU countries. Ukraine said normal service was restored on October 24. The reason and responsibility remain contested. Reuters reported, citing officials, that an order came from within the White House, although Trump denied personally issuing it. The disruption coincided with Washington’s pressure on Ukraine to stop striking Russian oil refineries.

For an ordinary user, losing Internet access can be an inconvenience. For armed forces, emergency responders and other essential services, it can have far more serious consequences. SpaceX also depends on American regulatory approvals and government contracts, giving Washington considerable leverage over the company. If American interests diverge from India’s during a crisis, this dependence could become a strategic vulnerability.

India needs alternatives

Keeping Starlink out entirely would sacrifice valuable connectivity, particularly in remote regions. India should retain its strict licensing conditions while improving its ability to check compliance. Starlink continues to evolve and regulation will have new challenges to contend with.

Therefore, a more effective way to reduce the dependency risks is to have alternatives to Starlink. Military and other essential users should maintain backup connections and tested procedures for switching between them. Using more than one satellite internet provider, from countries under different jurisdictions, would reduce dependence on the decisions of a single entity. Expanding ground coverage would reduce the number of communities for whom Starlink will be the only practical option. A parliamentary review found that only 30% of gram panchayats had a functional BharatNet connection as of February 2026.

But fibre and mobile towers cannot cover every ocean, mountain region or remote frontier. India will also need satellite alternatives of its own. India should encourage more domestic constellations and use their demand for satellites and rockets to build Indian industry. Industrial capacity and satellite Internet capacity go hand-in-hand. India needs enough alternatives to keep essential communications working even when Starlink is unwilling or unable to.
