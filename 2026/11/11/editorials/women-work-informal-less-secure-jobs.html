LABOUR · PLFS · GENDER · DATA POINT

More women work but in informal and less secure jobs

Tanu M. Goyal, Shravani Prakash, Ketaki Gaikwad & Arjun Tandon

Authors are with ICRIER. Views expressed are personal.

Data Point  |  November 11, 2026

Despite the rise of women’s participation in the labour force, women still carry the burden of unpaid domestic work and childcare

The rise in women’s labour force participation in India is now well-documented. Yet, it conceals a less encouraging reality — men and women are experiencing structural transformation very differently.

Between 2017-18 and 2025, the number of working women nearly doubled, from 104 million to 201 million, and the female worker population ratio rose from 22% to 38%. But most of the increase has come from rural India, largely in agriculture and in own-account and unpaid family work on farms and in household enterprises.

The positive here is that a larger part of women’s daily work is finally being counted. For decades, the labour women did on the fields and cattle sheds went mostly unrecorded, and was thereby left out of policy and budgets. Simply counting, however, has just revealed how many women work but says little about the work itself. The recent ICRIER report, ‘The Participation Paradox’, uses the Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) and the Time Use Survey to look at these dimensions. Five data points stand out.

Most of the surge in women’s employment has been absorbed by agriculture, the sector that structural transformation expects workers to move out of (Chart 1). The number of rural women working in crop and animal production rose from 54 million to over 100 million. Rural men, however, did move out as their share in agriculture fell from 54.1% to 47.2%, while women’s rose from 70.7% to 73.3%. Surprisingly, urban women employed in agriculture rose from 2.6 million to 6.3 million, becoming their largest single employer in 2025. Outside agriculture, women’s employment gains were concentrated in garments, retail and construction, sectors where work is largely informal and dominated by micro and own-account enterprises. The shift towards self-employment is particularly pronounced in rural areas where women in own-account work quadrupled, while the share of regular salaried jobs among them slipped from 10.5% to 9.3%.

Despite the rise in women’s workforce participation, average weekly working hours have fallen for every group between 2017-18 and 2025. The decline was steepest for rural women, who worked a little over six hours less per week. The combination of rising participation and falling hours suggests that much of the new employment is marginal and low in intensity (Chart 2).

Manufacturing reflects this pattern most clearly. In rural textiles, the gender gap in weekly working hours widened from 17 to 21; in wearing apparel, from 19 to 21. Garments and textiles are expected to draw women into regular factory work, where their hours would gradually rise towards men’s. The widening gap suggests that many women in these industries remain in home-based, piece-rate, or own-account work.

The wage gap

While women’s earnings have risen, men’s wages have risen faster (Chart 3). A rural woman now earns roughly 45% of what a rural man earns, down from 53%. The sectoral picture is equally telling. The widest hourly earnings gaps are in the sectors that employ most women: per hour, men in rural tobacco manufacturing earn 2.1 times what women do; in education, 2 times; in textiles, 1.8; in crop production, 1.7. By contrast, women earn on par with men in about 20 urban sectors, including scientific research, publishing, advertising and management consultancy, which employ a small fraction of women workers.

When asked why they are not in the labour force, 60% of women cite childcare and household commitments. Among men, fewer than 1% give these reasons. These patterns have barely changed since 2021-22. Skilling and job creation can expand women’s employment only if women have the time to take up these opportunities. That time is already absorbed by unpaid work at home that remains invisible in national income accounting. The Time Use Survey shows that in 2024, Indian women spent an average of 3.7 hours a day more than men on domestic work and nearly one hour more on care.

Concerningly, taking up paid work does little to ease this burden. Working women still spend around four hours a day on unpaid domestic work and nearly two on care, in rural and urban India alike. Working men, in contrast, spend under three hours on both types of activities combined.

These paradoxes suggest that the gains from India’s structural transformation have gone largely to men. Beyond raising participation, women should be pushed towards more productive, better-paid work without adding onto the unequal burden of unpaid care.

First, reduce that care burden, the main supply-side constraint. This means affordable, good-quality childcare, paid paternity and shared parental leave financed partly through social insurance. Second, make industrial and MSME policy work for women: MSME support should reward hiring women, widen access to finance and technology, and open hostels and arrange transport in textile parks and industrial areas. Third, create paid work for rural women close to home through place-based enterprise clusters, livelihood missions, and women-led producer organisations.

India has brought women’s work into the data. The next step is making that work more productive, better paid, and easier to access.
