The Nvidia affair and its likely repercussions

Jensen Huang’s push to sell more chips to China is over for now. Indian AI firms sourcing hardware from Nvidia may get hit

Director, Verda.ai; and Visiting Fellow, Centre for Policy Research and Governance.

Pravin Kaushal

The Hindu Business Line  |  November 11, 2026

Put the memo and the apology to one side for a moment and ask the question nobody is asking about the Nvidia scandal. Who came out of it ahead? Not Jensen Huang. On Sunday he told his staff he had been unfaithful to his wife. The reports say the woman is Alice Huang, a Chinese citizen who used to work at Megaspeed, a Singapore data centre company that has been under suspicion for a while of moving Nvidia chips into China. American intelligence is said to think she was working for China’s Ministry of State Security. By Monday night his board had opened an investigation, a shareholder had sued him in San Jose, his company had lost about $190 billion in a single day of trading, and President Trump had told reporters the FBI was looking into him.

Not Beijing, either, whatever it was hoping to get. The Foreign Ministry says the whole thing is a smear. The winners are a group of people in Washington who have spent two years trying to shut the gaps in America’s chip controls and kept bumping into the same man.

Huang has been the most effective opponent of those controls since they were written. Earlier this year he got the H200 chip approved for sale to China. There was one point he fought on more than any other. Two serving Commerce officials have told reporters that Nvidia pushed back hardest of all on remote access. That is the quiet road round the sanctions. A lab in China doesn’t need to import a chip if it can rent time on one sitting in a data centre in Singapore or Malaysia. Megaspeed, which has bought over $4.8 billion of Nvidia hardware since the middle of 2024, is in precisely that business. The rule was never written. Nvidia’s lobbying is a large part of why.

The machinery was turning

On the same day the memo went out, The New York Times published a different story altogether. Its reporters had gone to the Chinese companies that the Commerce Department had cleared to buy H200s. A lot of the addresses were empty rooms. Some of the firms turned out to be connected to sanctioned entities and to contractors for the Chinese military. So on one page, the boss of Nvidia had been compromised by someone from the company at the heart of the smuggling allegations. On the next, the approved Chinese buyers were fronts. You don’t need the two stories to be coordinated for them to add up to one conclusion: the approvals were a mistake and the Southeast Asian route has to be shut.

In late October the White House asked Congress to put a remote-access rule into the Overwatch Act and to narrow the MATCH Act so it catches advanced chipmaking and leaves older lines alone. Those two bills and the Chip Security Act may now ride into law attached to the defence budget. Lawmakers were telling Reuters that the scandal makes that more likely. And the man who would have stood in the way can’t.

National security officials have taken three things out of this week that they couldn’t get before: Huang’s credibility as a lobbyist; the H200 approvals and the remote-access loophole; and, through committees now going over Nvidia’s internal security, a seat at the table of the most valuable company in the world.

None of this is a long way from India. The loophole being closed is in our neighbourhood. If renting chips in Singapore and Malaysia becomes something you need a licence for, Indian AI companies that do exactly that, and the Indian data centres hoping to sell the same thing, will be caught in it. Our national AI plan assumes Nvidia keeps shipping. A company on a national security leash sends its chips where Washington wants them, not where the cheque comes from.

Huang may well come through this. The board is behind him, the President is behind him, and the company is still worth more than most countries. But the argument he spent two years making is over, and the gaps he fought to keep open are closing around him. The affair was the spark. The chip leak was always the target.
