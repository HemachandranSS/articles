INSOLVENCY · IBC · CORPORATE RESTRUCTURING

IBC rescue rests on ‘clean slate’

BASIC PRINCIPLE IGNORED. Rather than pursue extinguished claims, the IBC is meant to realise legitimate dues on time

Emeritus Fellow, Insolvency Law Academy; and Research Associate, Dr. Sahoo Regulatory Chambers.

MS Sahoo & Rupesh Choudhary

The Hindu Business Line  |  November 11, 2026

In the case of IBC resolutions, the government has pursued pre-CIRP liabilities, despite the law’s commitment to a clean slate.

On September 18, 2026, the Calcutta High Court directed the Income Tax Department to refund the amount it had adjusted against pre-CIRP (corporate insolvency resolution process) tax demands from a post-CIRP refund due to UltraTech Nathdwara Cement Ltd (Ultra). It also restrained the Department from initiating reassessment or other proceedings to recover pre-CIRP dues. In this case, the CIRP of Binani Cements Ltd (Binani) commenced on July 25, 2017. The National Company Law Appellate Tribunal approved the resolution plan on November 14, 2018, and the Supreme Court dismissed the challenge within five days. The resolution applicant took over on November 20, 2018; the company was renamed Ultra on December 13, 2018. The outcome was remarkable. Financial creditors recovered 100 per cent of their admitted claims of ₹6,469 crore. The resolution value exceeded three times the liquidation value. The IBC rescued a distressed business and maximised its value. Yet government departments continued pursuing pre-CIRP liabilities, forcing the company to defend its clean slate in court.

The Central GST Department raised demands for pre-CIRP tax liabilities and interest. The Rajasthan High Court quashed the demands. The Customs Department sought to continue a pending appeal concerning pre-CIRP liabilities; the Gujarat High Court abated the appeal. The Rajasthan Government’s Commercial Taxes Department refused to refund statutory pre-deposits against disputed VAT demands and amounts deposited under protest against disputed sales-tax demands. The Rajasthan High Court directed refunds, with applicable interest, in both cases. The State’s Mines and Petroleum Department raised demands for contributions to the District Mineral Foundation under the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957. The Rajasthan High Court set aside those demands.

These disputes concern different taxes, deposits and statutory demands, but all relate to pre-CIRP dues. The authorities had opportunities to submit claims during insolvency; some did so and contested the resolution outcome up to the Supreme Court. Yet departments pursued the underlying liabilities under their respective laws. The High Courts reiterated the settled legal position and held the demands arbitrary and illegal. Such litigation wasted public money, judicial time and business resources.

Section 31(1) makes an approved resolution plan binding on the Central Government, State Governments and local authorities. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that claims not forming part of an approved resolution plan stand extinguished and cannot subsequently be pursued against the successful resolution applicant. A business cannot be rescued if its new owner remains uncertain about its past liabilities. The Government itself acknowledged this principle.

On July 29, 2019, the Finance Minister assured Parliament that the IBC would prevail in the event of inconsistency with other laws and that the Government would make no further claims post-resolution. Yet, Ultra’s experience reveals a persistent gap between the law’s promise and its implementation by revenue departments. If departments can reopen extinguished claims years after resolution, the clean slate promise becomes illusory.

The state spends money pursuing unenforceable claims while risking the economic value the IBC was designed to preserve.

Applicants cannot reliably assess the liabilities they are taking on. They may lower their offers to account for endless government litigation or avoid rescuing businesses altogether. Creditor recoveries then fall, fewer distressed businesses are rescued, and investment, employment and productive capacity suffer.

Pursue legitimate dues

The alternative is not to abandon public revenue, but to pursue legitimate dues in time. The IBC gives the government a mechanism to do so, just as it has helped operational and financial creditors. When a company defaults on an operational debt owed to the Government, the appropriate authority can initiate insolvency proceedings. Government data indicate that 30,000 insolvency applications were withdrawn before admission, often following payment — illustrating how the credible prospect of insolvency can induce payment.

The Central and State Governments should issue binding instructions requiring every revenue department to act as a diligent creditor, before and during insolvency: identify and pursue legitimate dues promptly; participate effectively once insolvency begins; and respect the finality of binding resolution plans.

The Government must stop litigating against the clean slate and start using the IBC to its own advantage, rescuing distressed businesses while protecting public revenue.
