DATA CENTRES · ENERGY · WATER

Data centre dynamics

States must price in power and water

President, Danfoss India; and Chairman, CII Southern Region.

Ravichandran Purushothaman

The Hindu Business Line  |  November 11, 2026

Data centres are the factories of the digital economy, and India is building them faster than ever. India’s installed data centre capacity has grown from about 375 MW in 2020 to about 1,575 MW today, according to the Ministry of Electronics and IT. The Central Electricity Authority expects data centres to need about 17 GW of power by 2031-32.

Nearly ₹7 lakh crore is already being invested in India’s data centre sector, while several States are developing dedicated policies to attract further investments. Data centres bring construction jobs, skilled operations staff, local tax revenue and the digital backbone that start-ups, banks, hospitals and government services rely on. But they also bring two quiet demands that every State, and every citizen, should weigh: electricity and water.

Cooling story

Servers turn almost all the electricity they use into heat, and that heat must be removed every second of every day. Cooling can consume a quarter or more of a data centre’s power. Many facilities also cool by evaporating water. The arrival of artificial intelligence makes this more urgent. A traditional server rack drew 4-6 kilowatts; racks built for AI now draw 40 to over 100 kilowatts, and designs exceeding 500 kilowatts have been announced.

Better options exist and are already in use. Liquid cooling uses at least 15 per cent less cooling energy and can run in sealed loops that consume almost no water. Closed dry or adiabatic coolers use a fraction of the water of open cooling towers. Treated sewage water, rainwater and desalinated seawater can replace drinking water.

In 2026 the Bureau of Indian Standards adopted standard methods to measure a data centre’s power usage effectiveness (PUE), water usage effectiveness (WUE) and cooling efficiency. States hold the strongest levers: land, water, power tariffs and approvals. Five questions should be part of every data centre deal.

  • How efficient will it be, and will you prove it every year? Tie subsidies to measured, full-year PUE and WUE verified by an accredited auditor — Karnataka’s incentive after three years of proven low PUE is a good model.
  • Whose water will it use? No drinking water or groundwater for evaporative cooling in water-stressed blocks; Gujarat’s policy mandating desalination is instructive.
  • Where will the heat go? New facilities should export heat, with the pipes, pumps and heat exchangers in place.
  • Will the public see the numbers? Publish each facility’s energy and water performance every year.
  • Does it fit a national standard? Align with a proposed BEE star label for data centres, much like refrigerators and air conditioners.

Data centres can be good neighbours, but that depends on the questions States ask today, before the concrete is poured.
