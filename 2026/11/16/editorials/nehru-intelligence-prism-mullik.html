Looking at India’s first Prime Minister through an intelligence prism

Critics of Jawaharlal Nehru have spared no effort to portray him as being rosy-eyed and naive about China; yet, memoirs of Bhola Nath Mullik, the intelligence chief who served under Nehru for almost the entire duration of his Prime Ministership, make it clear this was far from the truth

Ramanathan Kumar

Op-Ed  |  November 16, 2026

Much has been written on Jawaharlal Nehru; Ramanathan Kumar adds a less-used angle — Nehru as seen by his long-serving intelligence chief, B.N. Mullik.

Mullik was Director of the Intelligence Bureau from 1950 until shortly after Nehru’s death. R&AW did not yet exist, so the DIB was the apex voice on intelligence and security.

An Indian Police officer, Mullik entered the IB in 1948 and became DIB two years later. Earlier, as ASP in Madhubani (Darbhanga), he tried to brief Nehru on police rehabilitation after Bihar’s 1934 earthquake; Nehru, thinking he was under surveillance, sent an angry note and Mullik resolved never to approach him again. A later IB report on minorities in East Pakistan that Nehru questioned deepened the chill. Sardar Patel then urged the new DIB to call on the Prime Minister — a meeting that reset the relationship: Nehru wanted unbiased intelligence, and Mullik valued his wider horizon of statecraft.

Critics cast Nehru as rosy-eyed on China; Mullik’s memoirs, read here as a study lens, push back on that picture.

Picture of a statesman

After retirement Mullik published a trilogy — Kashmir, the 1962 Indo-China war, and other national-security problems of the young republic. From that professional proximity comes a vivid picture of Nehru’s worldview and governing style.

Far from naivety, Mullik records Nehru warning as early as 1952 — around the first annual conference of State police chiefs — about an aggressive, militaristic China that used communist ideology to cloak hard national interest, and stressing the need to stay on guard.

On Sardar Patel’s November 1950 Tibet letter, the essay argues the common claim that Nehru ignored a prescient warning is overstated: under the circumstances the government pushed military readiness, border intelligence, and administrative reach adjoining Tibet. The contested forward policy — light police and intelligence checkpoints across claimed territory — was part of that multi-pronged response.

Different outlooks

Kumar contrasts Nehru’s effort to avoid war (to spare scarce resources after colonial drain and Partition) with Mao-era China’s readiness to treat force as normal statecraft while keeping a pretence of peace. Avoidance failed in 1962; Nehru sought U.S. armed help. Limited assistance came, but China — aiming to humble Nehru — pulled back from much of the Northeast while retaining gains in Ladakh.

Selective use of Bruce Riedel’s JFK’s Forgotten Crisis in Parliament to paint Nehru as panicking is set against Riedel’s own framing of India’s luck in having a humanistic founding leader. Mullik’s closing admiration — courage, patriotism, drive, determination, breadth of vision, even when he disagreed — is the essay’s final chord.

(The writer is former Special Secretary, R&AW. Views are personal.)
