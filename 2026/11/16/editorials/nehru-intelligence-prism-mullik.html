Looking at India’s first Prime Minister through an intelligence prism

Critics of Jawaharlal Nehru have spared no effort to portray him as being rosy-eyed and naive about China; yet, memoirs of Bhola Nath Mullik, the intelligence chief who served under Nehru for almost the entire duration of his Prime Ministership, make it clear this was far from the truth

Ramanathan Kumar

Op-Ed  |  November 16, 2026

Reading Jawaharlal Nehru through an intelligence lens, Ramanathan Kumar uses the memoirs of B.N. Mullik — DIB for almost the whole Nehru years, before R&AW existed — to rebut the charge that Nehru was rosy-eyed and naive about China.

Mullik joined the IB in 1948 and became DIB in 1950; early friction with Nehru after a 1934 Bihar earthquake episode gave way to a working relationship that encouraged frank reporting. His trilogy covers Kashmir, the 1962 war and wider security, and paints Nehru as a statesman with a broad view of statecraft.

Against the claim that Nehru ignored Sardar Patel’s 1950 Tibet warning, Mullik’s record shows stronger border intelligence, military readiness and the forward policy of police and intelligence posts in contested zones. Nehru, on this reading, saw an aggressive, militaristic China early, tried to avoid war so scarce resources stayed on economic development, yet prepared for force while keeping a public pretence of peace.

When China invaded in 1962, Nehru sought help from President Kennedy; Bruce Riedel’s JFK’s Forgotten Crisis is cited on that appeal. Kumar ends with Mullik’s lasting admiration for Nehru’s patriotism and vision despite disagreements. (The writer is former Special Secretary, R&AW. Views are personal.)
