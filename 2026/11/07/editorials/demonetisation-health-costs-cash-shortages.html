Demonetisation and the health costs of cash shortages

Research shows the hidden public health costs of India’s demonetisation and the importance of liquidity

Anindya S. Chakrabarti, Chirantan Chatterjee, Samarth Gupta

IIM Ahmedabad, University of Sussex, IIM Calcutta

On November 8, 2016, 86% of India’s currency by value — in the form of ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes — ceased to be legal tender at just four hours’ notice. The stated objectives included curbing counterfeit currency and black money and accelerating the adoption of digital payments. It was among the largest transitory liquidity shocks engineered in a cash-reliant economy. Nearly all demonetised currency returned — some 99.3% as per Reserve Bank of India data — and broader financial indicators such as interest rates and deposits remained stable. As Chodorow-Reich et al. documented in the Quarterly Journal of Economics (2020), the direct effect was a temporary reduction in the stock of money in the economy. Kenya (2019) and Nigeria (2022–23) later witnessed similar shortages. Yet the distributional welfare costs of such interventions remain under-studied — especially for essential goods purchased out of pocket.

Acute versus chronic, a natural lens

Our forthcoming research in the Journal of Economics & Management Strategy uses monthly sales data from the All India Organisation of Chemists and Druggists (AIOCD) covering 805 pharmaceutical firms, 31 regions and 18 illness categories. The natural experiment compares drugs for acute conditions — infections, pain, gastro-intestinal illness — with drugs for chronic ailments bought on predictable monthly schedules. Search-cost theory predicts that liquidity constraints bite harder on sudden, unplanned purchases for which consumers have not already found cheaper options. Sales volume of acute drugs fell by nearly 3.6% and value by 3.1% relative to chronic drugs around demonetisation. Product counts and the inter-quartile range of prices did not change, ruling out shortages and price spikes; prescription trends (IQVIA) and acute disease outbreaks (IDSP) showed no break. The decline was a pure intensive-margin contraction — households bought less of what remained on the shelf.

Lessons for the future

The result is not trivial luxury-cutting alone: in a system where out-of-pocket health spending is high, cash can bind essential purchases. Regions with weaker public health infrastructure saw larger relative declines in acute-drug sales, suggesting that primary-care access cushions liquidity shocks. Digital payments rose sharply after November 14, 2016, but cannot instantly substitute for every cash-dependent pharmacy transaction, especially for marginal households. Policy must weigh stated macro objectives against micro welfare losses before engineering large currency operations. Payment facilitation — timely credit lines to pharmacies, frictionless settlement for low-value medicines — can mitigate collateral damage when cash is withdrawn. Liquidity, we argue, is an economic determinant of health.

Need for better data

Observational evidence cannot rule out every mechanism, and pharmacy sales are not the same as morbidity or mortality. Still, the pattern is robust: acute care deferral, not chronic maintenance, bore the brunt. Linked consumption-health datasets — merging POS sales, insurance claims and outpatient records — would let regulators pre-register welfare protocols before monetary experiments. Future currency policy needs a health-sector checklist, not ex post inference from fragmented retail statistics. Measuring distributional impact upfront is cheaper than repairing trust in essential care after a cash squeeze.
