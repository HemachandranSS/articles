POLITY & RIGHTS · DISPLACEMENT

The struggle for justice is indivisible

From Panna to Delhi, the fight against displacement and disenfranchisement has a common thread — a struggle against state repression and the systematic erosion of a citizen’s constitutional rights

Brinda Karat

Senior leader, Communist Party of India (Marxist)

In the district jail in Panna, in Madhya Pradesh, a 24-year-old woman walked into the Superintendent’s office, her bright eyes alight as she stretched out her hands to greet us. She is Divya Ahirwar, an elected ward councillor from a Scheduled Caste-reserved seat, who has been in jail since September 27 on a fabricated charge of attempted murder. She has now been granted bail by the High Court of Madhya Pradesh in Jabalpur. Along with Amit Bhatnagar, founder of the Jai Kisan Sangathan, Divya has been at the forefront of a movement seeking justice for thousands of Adivasi and Dalit families facing displacement because of three projects in the State’s Panna and Chhatarpur districts. Amit, who undertook a hunger strike lasting several weeks, has also faced arrest. The three projects are the Majhgaya dam, the Runjh dam and the Ken-Betwa river-linking project. These are distinct projects, with different administrative histories and varying scales of displacement. But they share a common thread: the flagrant violation of the rights of project-affected families. The project sites have become centres of intense repression and brave resistance. The conflict raises fundamental questions about the power equations between the state, the class interests it represents and those against whom that power is exercised.

Water projects, displaced lives

At Majhgaya and Runjh, irrigation promises mask inadequate resettlement. Families report compensation revisions as late as December 2025 that still fail to restore livelihoods tied to forests and river banks. When officials pressed ahead, villagers launched the chita andolan — threatening funeral pyres to signal that displacement without dignity is a sentence of social death. The Ken-Betwa link, clearing forests in the Panna Tiger Reserve, threatens villages that the Forest Rights Act was meant to secure. Project authorities and a Hyderabad-based contractor, critics allege, treat gram sabha consent as a formality. Resettlement colonies lack schools, common property resources and viable employment — a pattern repeated wherever “development” is measured in cubic metres of water, not in lives rebuilt.

From Panna to Delhi, the fight against displacement and disenfranchisement has a common thread — a struggle against state repression and the systematic erosion of a citizen’s constitutional rights.

Judicial scrutiny

Petitions before the Supreme Court have challenged clearances that bypassed gram sabha mandates and wildlife law. Judicial scrutiny must ask not only whether files were signed but whether affected communities had free, prior and informed consent. Compensation without alternative livelihood is not rehabilitation. When movements raise these questions, the response has been arrests, fabricated cases and media silence. Divya’s incarceration was meant to decapitate local leadership; it has instead deepened solidarity with the Adivasi Adhikar Rashtriya Manch and allied groups that organised a mass protest at Jantar Mantar on October 2, 2026 — Gandhiji’s birth anniversary — to demand implementation of constitutional protections, not their bulldozing.

The many struggles

What is unfolding in Panna is part of an undeclared war on Adivasis across India: diluted forest clearance norms, criminalisation of protest, and welfare schemes held hostage to biometric databases. Article 21 — the right to life and livelihood — cannot mean survival without forest, water or voice. The state is running its savage bulldozer over constitutional rights, crushing lives, livelihoods and the very means of survival, while proclaiming “ease of doing business”. Justice for project-affected families, electoral integrity, labour rights and gender equality are not separate battles. They converge on one demand: that governments implement the Constitution they swear to uphold. The struggle for justice is indivisible; so must be the solidarity of those who defend it.
