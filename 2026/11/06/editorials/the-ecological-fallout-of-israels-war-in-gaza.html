INTERNATIONAL LAW · ENVIRONMENT & CONFLICT

The ecological fallout of Israel’s war in Gaza

The destruction of Gaza’s ecosystems underscores the need for accountability under international law

Abdullah Nasir

Associate Professor, Centre for International Legal Studies, Jawaharlal Nehru University

On November 5, 2001, the United Nations General Assembly (UNGA) designated November 6 each year as the International Day for Preventing the Exploitation of the Environment in War and Armed Conflict. This recognition underscores the far-reaching impacts of environmental damage caused by armed conflict, including degradation of ecosystems and resource depletion well after the conflict period. This impact often extends beyond the limits of national territories and affects future generations.

Considering the importance of the issue, the International Law Commission (ILC), in its report titled ‘Protection of the Environment in Relation to Armed Conflicts’ (PERAC), adopted 27 draft principles in 2022, which were formally noted by the UNGA on December 7, 2022. The overarching purpose of these principles is to enhance the protection of the environment in relation to armed conflicts. The first principle of the PERAC provides that the principles apply to the protection of the environment before, during and after an armed conflict, including in situations of occupation. In this context, Israel’s responsibility for internationally wrongful acts causing environmental harm arises not only from its ongoing genocide in Gaza but also from its continuing role as an occupying power.

The devastation in Gaza

On September 23, 2025, the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) released its second assessment of the environmental impact of the escalation of conflict in the Gaza Strip since October 2023. Drawing on satellite imagery and field observations by other UN agencies — UNEP staff could not enter Gaza — the report documents catastrophic damage. About 78% of buildings have been damaged or destroyed, generating roughly 61 million tonnes of debris, some possibly contaminated with asbestos and industrial waste. None of Gaza’s wastewater treatment plants were functioning; untreated sewage entered the environment. Most freshwater is contaminated. Vegetation loss is severe: about 97% of tree crops, 95% of shrubland and 82% of annual crops since 2023. Acute watery diarrhoea cases rose 36-fold. Coastal waters and marine life are likely contaminated by munitions, sewage and waste, though direct testing was impossible. The UNEP report does not capture the full extent of the environmental devastation in Gaza, as the situation continues to deteriorate.

The destruction of Gaza’s ecosystems underscores the need for accountability under international law.

Violations of environmental obligations

PERAC Principle 9 provides that states causing environmental damage in relation to armed conflict bear international responsibility and must make full reparation. As an occupying power, Israel is obligated to administer and use natural resources in the Occupied Palestinian Territory for the benefit of the protected population. The International Court of Justice, in its advisory opinion of July 19, 2024, acknowledged Israel’s systematic extraction of natural resources from the occupied territory. Such conduct violates environmental protection duties and infringes the Palestinian people’s permanent sovereignty over natural resources. The wartime devastation intensified since October 7, 2023 compounds pre-existing occupation-related harm. Accountability requires investigation, prosecution where applicable, and reparation measures that include debris management, rebuilding water and sewage systems, soil testing and restoration of agricultural capacity — not merely humanitarian relief disconnected from legal responsibility.

The overall picture of occupation

Environmental collapse in Gaza is inseparable from the broader architecture of occupation and conflict. Damage to soil, water and fisheries undermines long-term food security and public health across generations. International climate justice cannot be divorced from accountability for wartime environmental harm: the same communities facing displacement and bombardment bear the costs of desertified farmland and poisoned aquifers. Member states that supply arms or shield perpetrators from scrutiny become complicit in prolonging ecocide-like conditions. On the International Day for Preventing the Exploitation of the Environment in War and Armed Conflict, the lesson is not commemoration alone but enforcement — applying PERAC principles, supporting UN assessments, and insisting that occupying powers cannot treat ecosystems as expendable collateral. Without that, Gaza’s destruction foreshadows a world in which environmental law remains eloquent on paper and silent where it is most needed.
