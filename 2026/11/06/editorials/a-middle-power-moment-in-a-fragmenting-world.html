INTERNATIONAL RELATIONS · GLOBAL ORDER

A middle power moment in a fragmenting world

A weakening global order creates opportunities for middle powers to act as stabilisers and sustain international cooperation

Shashi Tharoor

Fourth-term Member of Parliament (Lok Sabha) from Thiruvananthapuram

When, early in October, this writer found himself in Berlin, addressing the question of whether this is a “Middle Power moment”, just a day after External Affairs Minister S. Jaishankar had made a speech in Delhi on the same topic, it led to a realisation why this theme has gained renewed salience this year. It began with Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney calling, in Davos in January, for the middle powers to collectively address the challenges facing the international system, and persisted with the global recognition that the world is no longer defined solely by the gravitational pull of superpowers, nor by the binary alignments of the Cold War, but by a wider constellation of states. These states neither command the international system nor submit passively to it. Instead, their influence derives from strategic positioning, economic weight, diplomatic agility, and normative ambition.

Middle powers are not easy to define. They are typically characterised by a combination of attributes: significant regional presence and influence, desire for greater relevance, growing economic capabilities, credible diplomatic networks, and the political will to act beyond their immediate neighbourhoods. They are not “great” powers in the traditional sense, but they possess enough weight to matter in global governance, and enough autonomy to pursue independent strategies. Their influence is exercised less through coercion than through coalition-building, agenda-setting, and the ability to act as bridges between different parts of the international system.

The middle power profile

There are many middle powers, and they are not a monolith. India, Brazil, Canada, Egypt, South Africa, Japan, Indonesia, Türkiye, Mexico and Germany are among the states frequently discussed in this company. What they share is not an identical worldview but a stake in an international order that is more representative and credible than the one bequeathed by 1945. Institutions such as the UN Security Council have not evolved adequately to reflect contemporary power shifts; middle powers are no longer content to be mere rule-takers, yet they are not rule-makers either. They seek a more equitable distribution of voice and influence, and their diplomacy is often pragmatic, multi-vector, and oriented to preserving strategic autonomy amid intensifying great-power rivalry.

A weakening global order creates opportunities for middle powers to act as stabilisers and sustain international cooperation.

Room to act

The erosion of great-power consensus, the paralysis or slow adaptation of multilateral institutions, and the fragmentation of global supply chains create both constraints and openings. Where Washington, Beijing and Moscow cannot agree, middle powers can convene issue-based coalitions — not a permanent bloc, but ad hoc cooperation on connectivity, climate finance, digital governance, or conflict mediation where rivals will not sit at the same table. India’s presidency of the G-20 in 2023, Brazil’s stewardship of climate diplomacy, and South Africa’s advocacy on vaccine equity illustrate how agenda-setting can proceed even when superpowers pull in different directions. Initiatives such as the Quad, BRICS, the India–Middle East–Europe Economic Corridor (IMEC), and the International North–South Transport Corridor show middle and great powers working in overlapping combinations. Yet this room to act is conditional: middle powers are more vulnerable to economic shocks than superpowers, and a state that hosts talks may suffer disproportionately if diplomacy fails.

The demand for cooperation

Global leadership is contested and often absent; someone must do the unglamorous work of sustaining cooperation. Middle powers cannot replace great powers, but they can moderate, mediate and innovate — acting as stabilisers in a fragmenting world. Their task is to reduce exposure to coercion while building assets others cannot easily substitute: convening power, trusted networks, and norm entrepreneurship in areas from climate to technology. The “middle power moment” is real, but fragile: if great powers repair their bargains or choose other intermediaries, the space shrinks quickly. For India and its peers, the lesson is to treat coalition-building as continuous diplomacy, not a one-time summit. In a world no longer defined by a single centre of gravity, those who bridge divides and keep channels open will matter as much as those who command armies or reserve currencies — provided they remember that influence is exercised by staying useful, not by pretending to be what they are not.
