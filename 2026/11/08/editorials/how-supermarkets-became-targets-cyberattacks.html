How supermarkets became targets of cyberattacks

John Xavier

November 8, 2026

In October, refrigeration temperatures at about 150 Intermarché stores in France rose sharply — later reports traced similar incidents across Belgium, Estonia, Finland, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Portugal, Germany, Sweden and other markets. No ransom was demanded and no customer data was stolen; attackers appeared intent on spoiling stock and alarming the public.

Modern supermarkets rely on central controllers connected to the internet for remote maintenance. Researchers found that exposed controllers could be issued a “forced defrost” command, stretching a cycle that normally lasts about an hour into as long as eight hours and pushing cabinet temperatures toward 50 degrees Celsius. The weak link is often legacy software on hardware that may have been in service for 10–20 years — including Danfoss controllers whose web interfaces Claroty flagged as vulnerable.

Vendors patch firmware and recommend firewalls, but operators frequently do not know what their chillers are connected to. Indian firms already sell IoT refrigeration monitoring to supermarkets and dark stores, raising the question whether similar attacks could occur here. Mitigations include replacing unsupported hardware, keeping controllers off the public internet, VPN access with multi-factor authentication, and mandatory security standards for food retail.
