JUMBO HUSTLE

The unseemly haste with which five elephants from Assam were transported to temples in Tamil Nadu raises questions about animal transfer rules and whether the process is cheat-proof

Geetha Srimathi

With inputs from B. Kolappan and Deepa H. Ramakrishnan  |  November 8, 2026

Five elephants — Durga, Hiralal (7), Shiva (5), Rupsing (13) and Bijuli (5) — recently reached Tamil Nadu temples via Chennai-based Seva Trust: Meenakshi Amman (Madurai), Arunachaleswarar (Tiruvannamalai), Ramanathaswamy (Rameswaram), Vaitheeswaran and Kamakshi Amman (Kancheepuram). The transfers unfolded with unusual speed, prompting scrutiny under the Wildlife (Protection) Act (2022 amendment) and the Captive Elephant (Transfer or Transport) Rules, 2024, which allow religious transfers but bar commercial trade.

The Wildlife Institute of India noted that genetic profiles of the animals were not established beforehand, raising doubts whether they were captive-bred or taken from the wild. Arun Prasanna (People for Cattle in India) said Assam’s history of capturing elephants survives through “gifts” and donations because outright sale is illegal. Shravan Krishnan (Besant Memorial Animal Dispensary) argued temple elephants are tamed, not domesticated: “Keeping them in captivity for life is akin to breaking their spirit.”

The [temple] elephants are not domesticated, they are tamed. Keeping them in captivity for life is akin to breaking their spirit. — Shravan Krishnan

Activists and the HR&CE Department face yawning enforcement gaps — from mahout training and genetic profiling to interstate paperwork — that allow humans to trade in pachyderms under the guise of devotion.
