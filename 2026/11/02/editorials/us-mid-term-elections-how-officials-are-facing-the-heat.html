U.S. mid-term elections: how officials are facing the heat

Decentralised counting, pre-emptive doubts over “honest” elections, and pressure on county officials who must certify results

Anil Raman

Retired Brigadier and Research Fellow, Geopolitics Program, Takshashila Institution

Americans vote tomorrow, November 3, to elect the Congress for the last two years of Donald Trump’s presidency. While Indian voters are used to a single counting day, with most results in by evening, a U.S. count can run for days, and in a close race for weeks. This year the worry is whether the outcome will be accepted at all. In 2020, doubts arose after the count, and Mr. Trump still has not accepted that defeat. This time, they are being raised before voting, with Mr. Trump stating that he will accept the result only “if the elections are honest”.

The U.S. does have a federal Election Assistance Commission (EAC), but its responsibility is limited to certifying voting machines and training election staff. The 50 States have thousands of county officials who man the booths, count the votes and declare results; India’s electronic voting machines can total crores of votes in a few hours. In the U.S., many votes come by post, and each envelope must be opened and its signature checked before the ballot is counted. Moreover, there is variance in the procedure followed by different States. California will accept a postal ballot up to a week after polling day if it was posted in time. Twenty-six States order an automatic recount when the margin is very narrow.

In several States, ballots cast in person on the polling day are reported first, and these tend to favour Republicans. Postal ballots, which have leaned Democratic, come in later and can wipe out an early lead. In 2020, Mr. Trump treated such a shift as proof of fraud.

Pre-emptive manoeuvring

The slow count matters more this year because of what precedes it. In July, the administration removed the last commissioners of the EAC, leaving it without any senior leadership. Two days earlier, the U.S. Department of Justice warned officials in all 50 States that they could be prosecuted over their voter lists. Before that, in Fulton county, Georgia, which is the county at the heart of Mr. Trump’s challenge to the 2020 result, the Justice Department demanded the names and contact details of everyone who had worked at the polls. A federal judge denied this demand and termed its scope “staggering”.

October has seen more such instances. The same department is now sending monitors to all 50 States, reportedly about 1,000 of them. This includes to six Democratic-leaning counties in Georgia. For the first time since 2002, Washington has not invited observers from the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe. On October 29, a senior Justice Department official sent a diktat to every State, reminding officials that failure to preserve election records is a crime carrying up to a year in prison. Justin Levitt, a former department official, said the letter “certainly looks like it’s meant to intimidate rather than to inform”.

On October 28, a federal judge in California rejected the Justice Department’s attempt to set aside about 20,000 ballots, writing, “these threats must stop”. Two days later, a judge in another case ordered the Department to stop threatening election officials with prosecution.

The most critical period begins after the polls end. In the U.S., county boards and then State officials must certify the count. For most of the country’s history, this was a formality. Since 2020, it has become a battleground because a refusal or delay in certifying can hold up a result while lawyers and politicians fight over it. States certify results during November and December, and the newly elected Congress takes its seats only on January 3, 2027.

On October 22, Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche was asked whether the Justice Department might intervene in certification. He said it had no plan to “improperly interfere”, thereby declining to rule it out. A week later, the Department displayed this stance. In an amended lawsuit, it sought to block the certification of Alaska’s Senate result if Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan loses narrowly. The complaint rests on a claim that Democrats recruited a spoiler, a retired teacher also named Dan Sullivan — an allegation that the campaign of Democratic challenger, Mary Peltola, denies. Such a move would raise serious constitutional questions, since elections are run by the States.

While recounts and lawsuits are expected in any close race, the worry is about officials setting aside a legal and correct count, especially in seats which witness close contests.

Why the system may prevail

Overturning a U.S. election result, however, would need multiple officials in many counties to compromise. In 2020, the staff held and did not succumb to pressure. County officials across the country stood by their totals, and Republican officials in key States certified results that went against Mr. Trump. This year, courts, including judges he appointed, have already refused several of his administration’s demands. Whether officials hold firm again, under heavier pressure, will be what the next two months show.

India has a different election system that depends on public trust in the integrity of a single Election Commission. This may bring speed and uniformity but it rests heavily on the credibility of the three commissioners and their selection. This year’s special revision of electoral rolls, which critics say left out many eligible voters, shows how quickly that trust can be strained. In both democracies, the argument has shifted from the ballot box to the voter list and the referee. India would do well to watch how U.S. referees hold up in the weeks after November 3.
