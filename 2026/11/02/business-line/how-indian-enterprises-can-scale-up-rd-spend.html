OPINION · R&D & INDUSTRIAL POLICY

How Indian enterprises can scale up R&D spend

Raising deduction for in-house R&D and identifying strategic sectors are vital, especially for smaller firms

Reji K Joseph

Associate Professor, Institute for Studies in Industrial Development, New Delhi  |  November 2, 2026

Recent Department of Science and Technology reports show India’s Gross Expenditure on R&D (GERD) rising from 0.71% of GDP in 2013-14 to 0.84% in 2023-24, with 0.9% projected for 2025-26 — a level India had already touched in 2008-09. Ambition to become a developed economy in two decades needs a faster “technology take-off,” not incremental drift.

Two patterns stand out. First, business enterprise R&D (BERD) rose from 43.6% of GERD in 2014-15 to 49.3% in 2023-24. Second, multinational and large Indian firms dominate BERD — 71.3% in 2023-24. Korea and China draw more than 70% of GERD from business; without a similar shift, a 2% GERD-GDP ratio stays out of reach.

Most OECD economies moved from below 1% to 2% in about a decade. South Korea crossed 2% in the early 1990s; China did so by 2013. India’s task is to jump from 0.84% to 2% by about 2036.

Driven by large firms

BERD is highly concentrated: transportation and pharmaceuticals alone account for 54% across 41 industry groups. Tata Motors, Mahindra & Mahindra and a handful of pharma majors anchor the league tables — ten of 17 Indian firms in the EU Industrial R&D Investment Scoreboard (2025) sit in these two sectors. Yet top Indian pharma names show R&D intensity (R&D-to-sales) around 8%, versus ~22.4% for global leaders. Large firms alone cannot carry the 2% target.

Smaller firms can be far more R&D-intensive. In scientific instruments, private firms with average turnover of about ₹45 crore posted 23.2% intensity in 2023-24, against 3.39% for transport majors (₹8,130 crore average turnover) and 5.22% for pharma (₹1,006 crore).

What is to be done?

Restore and tilt fiscal incentives: weighted deduction for in-house R&D was cut from 200% (2010-11) to 150% and then 100%; evidence shows SMEs respond strongly, as in Korea’s tiered R&D tax credits (12–25% for SMEs versus 1–15% for large firms). Pair this with mission-oriented sector policies — as Korea did in 1982 and China in 2006 — for quantum, semiconductors and AI, using the ₹1 lakh crore RDI scheme as financing arm.

Raising GERD to 2% by 2036 requires BERD to climb from 0.42% to about 1.4% of GDP, growing roughly twice as fast as nominal GDP. That demands broad-based BERD growth, not concentration in a few champions.
