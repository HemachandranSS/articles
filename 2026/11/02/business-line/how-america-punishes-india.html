How America punishes India

Why is every episode of capital flight preceded by India annoying America?

TCA Srinivasa Raghavan

The Hindu Business Line  |  November 2, 2026

As foreign portfolio investors pull out and the rupee weakens, market commentators increasingly pin the slump on the 2024 hike in long-term capital gains (LTCG) tax and on securities transaction tax (STT). That is a textbook example of post hoc, ergo propter hoc: a single causal story for a problem that is plainly more complex.

Episodes of capital flight from India and rupee depreciation have, historically, often coincided with periods when New Delhi has irritated Washington — what the column calls annoying Uncle Sam.

The timeline is familiar: refusal of U.S. investment in Indian industry in the 1950s; opposition to the Vietnam War in the 1960s; going nuclear in the 1970s; limited practical support in the 1990 Gulf crisis; the 1998 tests; Manmohan Singh’s promises to a sceptical Congress; Barack Obama’s undisguised annoyance; and, more recently, a dust-up over Donald Trump’s self-description as mediator with Pakistan. Each phase brought its own diplomatic friction — and, the author argues, its own market stress.

When it wants to, America can cause severe economic damage to India by squeezing the supply of dollars to us. That’s all it takes.

Foreign institutional investors (FIIs) do respond to tax and valuation, but they also respond to geopolitical signalling, reserve adequacy and the ease of repatriating dollars. Domestic bulls who demand LTCG rollbacks as a silver bullet may be fighting the wrong battle if the deeper driver is trust in stable dollar liquidity and cordial ties with the world’s reserve-currency issuer.

None of this excuses weak macro management or overpriced markets. It does caution against mistaking correlation in time for causation in policy — especially when forex reserves, oil shocks and global risk appetite are also in the mix.
