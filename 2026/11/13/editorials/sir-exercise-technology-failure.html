The SIR exercise is also a technology failure

Priya Seetharaman  |  November 13, 2026

Public debate on the Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of India’s electoral rolls (2025-26) has focused on law and politics. Less noticed is a technology failure: the centralised digital platform used for the exercise lacked the piloting, iteration and published specifications that mark successful Indian digital public infrastructure such as IRCTC, UPI and Aadhaar.

Age and name discrepancies

Voters were flagged for “logical discrepancies” by ERONet using rigid filters — such as parent-child age gaps — that lack context sensitivity when ages on Indian rolls are often approximate. An analysis by The Hindu found family-size checks in Delhi flagged Muslim electors at a higher rate. Matching names across scripts (for example, Bengali rolls digitised into English) compounded errors.

Overarching power

Software changed frequently while SIR was under way. Booth Level Officers faced shifting protocols and new features without documentation or a change-freeze window. Options were often explained via informal videos or WhatsApp rather than updated manuals. When code changes but manuals do not, the software becomes the rulebook — and digital workflows sometimes demanded details (such as grandparent information on Form 6) beyond statutory requirements.

Questions of timing

Deadlines followed the electoral calendar, not system readiness. In West Bengal, over half-a-crore names were marked “under adjudication” in the final roll. Invoking Lawrence Lessig’s insight that code is law, the author warns that rules written into software decided outcomes the statutes did not — putting voting rights at risk.
