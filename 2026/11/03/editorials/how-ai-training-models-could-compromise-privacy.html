How AI training models could compromise privacy

Credible studies have shown that querying an AI model with ‘anonymised’ data to extract personal details that it had memorised during training can be used to identify individuals and build their profiles

Tulika Avni Sinha

Senior Research Fellow (Data Governance, Privacy and AI), World Privacy Forum

The right to privacy is a constitutional right upheld by the Supreme Court in 2017. However, the legal instruments currently in place to safeguard this right seem inadequate, especially considering the technological revolution led by Artificial Intelligence (AI) around the world. While AI regulation is one of the most commonly discussed issues within governments today, the recommendations have been broad, vague and have only led to further questions. AI has immense potential to boost productivity and have positive effects on people’s lives; any indiscriminate regulatory or policy measure risks inhibiting innovation and economic growth. However, there are lower hanging fruits; specific gaps that can be filled to better safeguard people’s constitutional rights without permanently losing out in the AI race.

The Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act was passed in 2023, with the DPDP Rules being notified in November 2025. The implementation is phased with some provisions on consent and safety becoming effective only in 2027. Prior to the DPDP Act, the primary legal instrument to regulate the use and management of personal data was the Information Technology (IT) Act, 2000. Together the IT Act, 2000 and its 2011 Rules broadly regulated the export of “sensitive personal data” on the condition that the recipient ensured a level of data protection required under Indian law, and that the transfer was either necessary to perform a lawful contract or had been consented to by the data provider. An assertion and claim were sufficient to record adequacy. The criminal penalty for unauthorised disclosure was capped at three years’ imprisonment or a fine of ₹5 lakh. These penalties only applied to disclosure of sensitive personal data that were listed in the Rules such as financial data, medical or health details, sexual orientation, biometric information etc. The Rules did not apply to personal data such as names, addresses, phone numbers, caste, religion, political affiliations, browsing history, social media activity, online or offline behavioural patterns, institutional affiliations etc.

Privacy coverage

Viewed against the demands of the AI age, the DPDP framework leaves large volumes of personal information under-protected. The Act does not apply to personal data that is publicly available, or to information inferred from a user’s publicly available parameters on social media — names, gender, location, organisation, images, videos, and the emotional states, mobility and behavioural tendencies that algorithms can derive from them. India is the largest market for Facebook and Instagram; Meta, Amazon and Google train AI systems on user-generated data at a scale few other countries can match. Public accessibility should not be treated as permission for unrestricted commercial exploitation: when such data are aggregated and modelled, seemingly harmless details can expose highly sensitive aspects of a person’s life.

Unlike the IT Rules, the DPDP Act does not carve out a distinct category of sensitive personal data. Passwords, health information and biometrics receive the same broad treatment as a city name or shopping preferences — a regressive dilution of protections that earlier law accorded to categories capable of direct privacy harm. Scraping publicly posted material for model training likewise falls outside the Act’s coverage, even though the combined use of non-sensitive datapoints can disclose sensitive information when analysed with other variables.

Training AI on personal data

The DPDP Act does not discuss how AI models are trained on personal data. Yet one of the most serious emerging risks is memorisation: models can regurgitate information they absorbed during training. Personal data converted into an anonymised training set may still be queried to retrieve details about identifiable individuals.

Credible studies have shown that querying an AI model with ‘anonymised’ data to extract personal details that it had memorised during training can be used to identify individuals and build their profiles.

Studies also indicate that sensitive attributes can be inferred from ordinary public information through AI — emotional conditions, mobility, relationships and behavioural patterns. Indian users presently lack an effective right to object to their data being used for AI training. Substantive consent obligations under the DPDP framework are phased, with several provisions becoming enforceable only by May 2027; even thereafter, publicly available personal data used for training may remain difficult to challenge. Meta offers users in the European Union an opt-out from certain uses of their information in AI training; Indian users enjoy no comparable mechanism.

The Act recognises a right to erasure, but chiefly for data for which the user gave consent, and it is unclear whether the right extends to information inferred from such data. An exemption for processing for research, archival or statistical purposes — with rules yet to be fully specified — creates ambiguity over whether AI training to “improve services” can be classified as research and thereby escape obligations. Erasing data from a trained model is technically distinct from deleting a database row; without clarity, individuals may secure erasure on paper while a model continues to reproduce what it has learned.

What can be done

India need not respond with sweeping bans on AI training or indiscriminate curbs on data flows that would undermine inclusion, efficiency and representation of the country’s diversity in model performance. Targeted amendments and rules can close the gaps: extend DPDP protections to personal data used to train AI models, including scraped public data and inferred attributes; provide a meaningful right to object to such training; restore a sensitive personal data category with stricter processing requirements; strengthen erasure by requiring fiduciaries to disclose other entities holding a person’s data where feasible; and clarify whether AI training falls within the research exemption. Public concerns are documented; delay in plugging these holes will leave constitutional privacy rights increasingly hollow as AI is embedded across government, business and civil society.
