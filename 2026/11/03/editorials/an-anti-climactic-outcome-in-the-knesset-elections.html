INTERNATIONAL RELATIONS · WEST ASIA & ISRAELI POLITICS

An anti-climactic outcome in the Knesset elections

While voters have reprimanded Benjamin Netanyahu for his flawed conduct, they seem to be willing to give him another chance

Mahesh Sachdev

Retired Indian Ambassador, specialising in West Asia and oil issues

For the Israeli Knesset election hyped as “consequential” and a “day of reckoning for the October 7 Hamas attack”, the verdict points to new trends and the normalisation of trenchant nationalism. The inconclusive outcome risks disappointing most stakeholders. Israel, being at the epicentre of the ongoing regional tumult, needs a real-time analysis.

Israel’s total electorate is 7.3 million. Yet it has 38 officially recognised political parties vying for 120 Knesset seats, of which only 11 parties are likely to cross the 3.25% voting threshold needed to enter the legislature. The elections are on a proportional representation basis. The parties are currently arrayed in three coalitions: the ruling extreme right, the centrist bloc and the Israeli-Arab bloc which got 53, 48 and 19 seats, respectively. The last group representing a 21% minority is largely untouchable for mainstream Jewish parties. The electorate is also split along ethnic and origin-based lines into occidental, oriental and orthodox cohorts. Since the last elections in 2022, nearly half a million new voters have come of age. The voter turnout jumped by nearly a tenth to 77.7% — a sign of sharp political engagement.

None of the blocs reached the 61-seat majority mark in the 120-member house. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s Likud remains the largest single party, and with allies such as United Torah Judaism, Shas and Otzma Yehudit, the nationalist-religious bloc totals 53 seats. The anti-Netanyahu “change” camp centred on former army chief Gadi Eisenkot’s Yashar and other parties stands at 48; Arab-led lists, including the Joint List and Ra’am, won a record 19. On paper, a centrist–Arab combination could cross 61, but the political taboo against Jewish–Arab governing pacts shrinks the real coalition pool to 101 Jewish seats, favouring prolonged bargaining and repeated kingmaker demands from smaller parties.

A war hungry rule

The election was the first since the October 7, 2023 Hamas attack and unfolded amid multi-front conflict involving Hamas, Hezbollah, the Houthis and Iran. Voters punished accountability gaps — piecemeal military inquiries, hostages lost or traded in negotiations, and adversaries “decimated” yet unbowed. Security failures and war fatigue weighed heavily, alongside domestic controversy over judicial overhaul moves and Mr. Netanyahu’s corruption trial. Yet the right bloc still leads: anger at the Prime Minister has often translated into shifts within the nationalist camp rather than a wholesale transfer of power. President Isaac Herzog is likely to task Mr. Netanyahu first with forming a government; failure would pass the mandate to the opposition, and repeated deadlock could force yet another election.

While voters have reprimanded Mr. Netanyahu for his flawed conduct, they seem to be willing to give him another chance.

Israel’s wars have imposed steep economic and social costs and dented its international standing, including legal scrutiny over conduct in Gaza. An extended caretaker administration, should coalition talks stall, could further vitiate the regional atmosphere and narrow prospects for diplomatic off-ramps in Lebanon, the occupied territories and with Iran.

A dangerous shift

Half a million young voters entering the rolls since 2022 have reinforced a hardened nationalist mainstream. Arab citizens — 21% of the population and now 19 seats strong — cannot be politically ostracised, yet remain excluded from viable coalition math for most Jewish parties. That paradox encapsulates the verdict: higher participation and a stronger Arab voice, but no breakthrough toward compromise formulas on Gaza, the West Bank, Lebanon or Iran. Trenchant nationalism is normalised rather than fringe.

For India, stability in Tel Aviv still matters for defence, technology and energy ties nurtured over decades, even as New Delhi maintains its stated positions on a two-state path. Whoever heads the next coalition will likely preserve government-to-government continuity with New Delhi; what changes is the regional risk environment — trade disruption, diaspora safety, and port investments such as Haifa — if war-first politics persist without a durable settlement. Israel has exercised democracy vigorously; the anti-climactic hung parliament suggests governability may lag behind engagement.
