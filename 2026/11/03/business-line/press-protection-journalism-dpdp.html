Journalism needs carve-out in data privacy law

Editorial Desk

The Hindu Business Line  |  Tuesday, November 3, 2026

Investigative journalism often processes personal data without consent to expose corruption and hold power to account. The Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023 does not offer a clear statutory exemption tailored to bona fide journalistic activity — a gap that publishers and editors cannot paper over with editorial codes alone.

Section 7 lists “legitimate uses” permitting processing without consent — state functions, employment, medical emergencies, court orders and the like — but not public-interest reporting. Section 12 grants data principals rights to correction and erasure. News archives that remain online indefinitely may face takedown demands that collide with the public record function of the press unless journalism is shielded in rules.

Section 36 empowers the Government to require data fiduciaries to furnish information — raising risks for confidential sources if law-enforcement requests are not bounded. The Digital News Publishers Association has challenged related provisions in the Supreme Court, arguing the statutory framework is skewed against publishers.

Comparators are not imaginary: Article 85 of the EU’s GDPR and the UK Data Protection Act 2018 recognise journalistic purposes with tailored exemptions balancing privacy and press freedom. India’s rules should clarify consent requirements for news-gathering, limits on erasure in published reports, and source protection when agencies seek data from media organisations — without turning the Act into a charter for unaccountable surveillance of newsrooms.

Key Terminologies

  1. Section 7 Legitimate Uses (No Journalism Carve-Out) (சட்டம் பிரிவு 7)
  2. Section 12 Erasure vs News Archives (அழிப்பு உரிமை)
  3. Section 36 Government Information Powers (அரசு தகவல் அதிகாரம்)
  4. GDPR Article 85 Journalistic Exemption (பத்திரிகை விலக்கு)
