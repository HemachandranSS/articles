Building immunity to shocks

India has weathered oil shocks emanating from Middle East tensions. This resilience must be turned into advantage.

Ashima Goyal

Professor Emeritus, IGIDR  |  November 3, 2026

Escalating Middle East tensions have again sent oil markets into poly-shocks that hit oil importers hard. Yet India’s macro performance in the first half of the year exceeded many forecasts — a sign that buffers built over the past decade are working, even if the work is unfinished.

Energy intensity is falling, alternative sources are gaining share, and large refining capacity helps absorb crude volatility. Counter-cyclical excise and oil pricing choices, improving tax buoyancy and gradual fiscal consolidation have widened policy space. Supply-side reforms and better-quality public expenditure matter as much as shock absorption; the IMF’s upward revision to Indian growth forecasts reflects that combination.

Current oil prices may have crossed the red flag of $100, but in real terms they are much below levels reached in past oil shocks, even as their impact on cost of production has fallen.

Policy design

Resilience is not free: forex reserves and strategic interventions carry opportunity costs, and fiscal space must be preserved without starving essential spending. The question is whether resilience becomes competitive advantage — through FTAs, services exports and a lower equilibrium current account deficit — or merely crisis insurance.

Resilience: costs or benefits?

Holding large FX reserves stabilises the rupee but ties up resources with low returns. Gains from resilience show up when current account deficits (CAD) stay manageable, inflation expectations remain anchored, and local governments deliver services that make growth feel inclusive. Institutionalised decentralisation and accountability at the local level turn macro strength into citizens’ lived security.
