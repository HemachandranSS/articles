The potential of India’s clean power ambition

POWER UP. Cheap batteries for storage, low daytime tariffs, easy grid access will cut power shortage, costs

Faculty Directors at the India Energy and Climate Center, UC Berkeley’s Goldman School.

Nikit Abhyankar & Amol Phadke

The Hindu Business Line  |  November 12, 2026

Prime Minister Narendra Modi has put 900 gigawatts (GW) of non-fossil power capacity by 2035, mostly solar and wind, firmly on India’s clean-energy agenda. The ambition is reflected in transmission investment plans for the next decade.

This raises three questions. First, how can these goals address power shortages and expensive power purchases by utilities? At present, about 40 GW of low-cost solar projects are unable to sell their power to discoms. Second, can a system dominated by solar and wind deliver reliable electricity at lower cost? Third, will the transition strengthen India’s energy security and industry, or create new dependencies on imported technologies and critical materials?

The first puzzle is striking because deployment is accelerating. Industrial and commercial consumers buying electricity directly drive substantial growth: around 8 GW of open-access solar came online in 2025. Yet about 40 GW of auctioned projects remain without buyers. Solar-rich afternoons bring abundant cheap power, sometimes at near-zero exchange prices. After sunset, Indian Energy Exchange prices have repeatedly touched ₹10 per unit, with Tamil Nadu paying up to ₹37, while some States have faced shortages.

Addressing the mismatch

Two solutions address this mismatch. Dramatically cheaper battery storage makes daytime solar a cost-effective option for evening and nighttime demand. Second, significantly lower daytime tariffs can encourage demand to shift to solar hours. Cheap daytime electricity could replace expensive imported gas for industrial heating, while solarising agricultural feeders through PM-KUSUM creates daytime demand.

Before signing 25-30-year coal contracts, utilities should assess renewables, storage, demand flexibility.

SECI’s 1,000-MW “thermal-mimic” auction contracted a coal-like supply profile from renewables and storage at ₹5.25–5.26 per unit, fixed for 25 years — roughly ₹3.8 per unit adjusting for inflation. Analysis suggests around 200 GW of storage, transmission expansion and flexible demand could be critical for integrating 900 GW by 2035. A least-cost clean-power pathway could cut average procurement costs by around 15% in real terms, saving over ₹2 lakh crore annually by 2035. Over 70% of lifetime economic value of batteries could remain domestic even with imported cells.

Action plan

Five actions are needed: favour cheaper renewables and storage over delayed coal projects through technology-neutral auctions; ease grid connections by leveraging underutilised nighttime grid access; let consumer prices reflect the cost of supply with deep solar-hour discounts; let large consumers choose suppliers by default; and combine smart imports, domestic manufacturing and recycling of critical materials.
