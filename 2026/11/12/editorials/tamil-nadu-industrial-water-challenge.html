TAMIL NADU · WATER · INDUSTRY

Tamil Nadu’s growing industrial water challenge

Rida Wanbha Nongbri & Yukesh Saravanan

Assistant Professor; and Researcher at the Madras Institute of Development Studies.

Op-Ed  |  November 12, 2026

Tamil Nadu is one of India’s leading investment destinations, thanks to its skilled workforce, strong manufacturing base and enterprise-friendly policies. Reflecting its investment-friendly credentials, NITI Aayog’s Investment Friendliness Index (IFI), released in July 2026, ranks the State third, behind Gujarat and Maharashtra. The first-of-its-kind report assesses States’ readiness to attract and sustain investments. For Tamil Nadu it identifies limited international air connectivity, Chennai Port infrastructure constraints and inadequate industrial water supply in landlocked and rain-shadow districts as key lacunae.

Without compromising on water security, can Tamil Nadu expand its industrial base, albeit with a high MoU conversion rate, 22% better-than-average Air Quality Index score and strong disaster preparedness perception scores?

With its robust and decentralised industrial economy, the State houses 130 Global Fortune 500 companies across 110 industrial parks, flanked by 42-plus operational Special Economic Zones. Notwithstanding global uncertainties, the State continued attracting Foreign Direct Investments even as overall investment at the national level slackened. While India’s total FDI declined between 2022-23 and 2023-24 from $46.03 billion to $44.42 billion, the State’s inflows accelerated to peak at $3.681 billion in 2024-25.

A State with limited water

Tamil Nadu’s economy has long since outgrown its water reserves. Much of the State is located in the rain-shadow region of the Western Ghats. With an average annual rainfall of 960 mm, the State accounts for only 3% of India’s water resources. Surface water potential is estimated at 853 TMC. A 2025 joint assessment by the Central Ground Water Board and the State’s Water Resources Department shows groundwater extraction at about 73.5% of the extractable resource. Industrial districts such as Salem, Vellore and Chennai are classified as over-exploited, while Krishnagiri and Coimbatore fall under the critical category. Industrial water demand, estimated at 1,555 MCM in 2001, is projected to rise to 1,985 MCM by 2050.

Weak water security threatens to undermine the State’s investment appeal and industrial growth prospects.

Managing water better

Tamil Nadu does not need to find more water as much as it needs to manage the water it already has better. One practical step is to price industrial water according to local water availability — not as a punitive cost to industry but as an investment in long-term water security. In June 2026 alone, 28 industries in the State were issued closure orders under the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974.

The zero liquid discharge (ZLD) system helps recover up to 90% of the water required, and textile clusters in Tiruppur are a case in point. A concerted effort to integrate green energy and water security could make ZLD, desalination and sewage treatment more affordable for industrial use.

Growth needs water security

Paradoxically, as Tamil Nadu’s industrial future becomes increasingly tied to its water economy, the Water Resources Department faces an 8.06% cut in its allocation this year, from ₹10,076 crore in the Interim Budget to ₹9,264 crore. The recently passed Tamil Nadu Water Resources (Regulation, Management and Augmentation) Bill, 2026, awaits the Governor’s assent. Better pricing, stronger water governance and prudent consumption are essential. It is high time to treat water as an infrastructure that must be built and paid for.
