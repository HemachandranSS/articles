CRITICAL MINERALS · EV · CIRCULAR ECONOMY

Turning battery waste into strategic mineral wealth

Shyamasis Das

Fellow at the Centre for Social and Economic Progress. Views expressed are personal.

Op-Ed  |  November 12, 2026

In his concluding remarks at the 18th BRICS Summit in New Delhi on September 13, 2026, Prime Minister Narendra Modi warned against the “weaponisation of technology and critical minerals”. His remarks assume significance amid rising global demand for critical minerals such as lithium, nickel and cobalt. As transport and other energy-intensive sectors increasingly shift towards electrification, the demand for electricity, lithium-ion batteries and electrical conductors is also growing. Their ores are either scarce or their supply chains geographically concentrated, leaving these critical minerals vulnerable to geopolitical tensions, resource nationalism and trade restrictions. China currently dominates critical mineral processing. For India, which is heavily dependent on imports, securing reliable supplies has therefore become a strategic priority.

A resource closer to home

The Government of India is pursuing international partnerships and domestic exploration to secure critical mineral supplies. It must also tap a growing resource available in its backyard: minerals recovered through recycling. End-of-life (EoL) lithium-ion batteries can be treated either as waste or as a “waste-to-wealth” resource. Yet when products reach EoL, their batteries often end up in landfills or informal recycling channels run by kabadiwalas. Material recovery rates are reportedly just 10%–20%, compared with over 90% in formal recycling facilities.

A circular battery economy can help secure India’s critical mineral supplies.

With more than 8.7 million EVs running on Indian roads by FY 2025-26, the volume of spent batteries is expected to rise. Analysis shows recycling could meet about 10% to 12% of India’s lithium demand for EV battery manufacturing. India’s Battery Waste Management Rules, 2022, provide an important foundation through extended producer responsibility, but capacity needs to be planned ahead of the expected surge.

The strategy to adopt

First, recycled critical minerals should be incorporated into strategic resource planning and the proposed national critical mineral stockpile. Second, industry and research labs should intensify R&D in mineral recycling technologies. Third, India needs standards for battery characterisation, recycled mineral quality and second-life applications. Fourth, EV manufacturers should offer remunerating take-back or buy-back options. Finally, battery recyclers need patient capital for facilities with long gestation periods.

Preparing for the battery surge

India should not wait for millions of retired EV batteries to accumulate before deciding what to do with them. Done well, battery recycling can turn an EoL liability into a domestic resource, create a new industrial ecosystem and provide a modest but meaningful buffer against critical mineral supply shocks.
