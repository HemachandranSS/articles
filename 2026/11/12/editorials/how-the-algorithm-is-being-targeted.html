How the algorithm is being targeted

Raviesha Pradeep

Spokesperson for the Cockroach Janta Party.

Op-Ed  |  November 12, 2026

On July 23, journalist Ila Kazmi posted a reel in which one could see a Delhi Police van at Jantar Mantar using facial recognition technology to match protesters’ faces in real time. By the next day, the reel was unavailable in India, and on September 9, her Instagram account was withheld. Instagram said that it was complying “with a legal request”. On October 9, the Delhi High Court in Ila Kazmi vs Union of India asked the Central government, Delhi Police, and Meta to produce the orders behind both blocks. Between March and July, government agencies sent nearly 1.95 lakh blocking orders to Instagram, Facebook, and YouTube — one every 68 seconds. Most went through the Sahyog portal, which lets Ministries, State governments, and the police issue takedowns directly. Since February, platforms have had three hours to comply with any takedown request issued through Sahyog.

When a post is blocked, the user at least gets a notice. When a post is quietly pushed down the feed due to skewed algorithmic design, there is nothing to challenge.

The timing of the spike in takedown orders also matters. The Cockroach Janta Party (CJP) began on May 16, and within four days it had crossed nine million followers on Instagram, overtaking the BJP’s official handle. On May 21, its X handle was withheld in India. On July 24, four days after the Jantar Mantar protest, Prime Minister Narendra Modi reportedly told his Cabinet to get more active on Instagram.

The Insta rise

On October 10, the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) wrote to Meta asking it to review its “recommendation and content ranking mechanisms” so that they do not cause “disproportionate exposure of users to a single or limited set of narratives”. This makes it clear that the algorithm is also a target now. In Ahmedabad, Shrey Gupta, a 28-year-old engineer who built a website to collect videos of police action at Jantar Mantar, said he was questioned for four hours; an officer told him that protesting was not the problem, posting about it was.

The Karnataka High Court on September 24, 2025, dismissed X Corp’s challenge and called Sahyog “far from being constitutional anathema”. However, this runs counter to the Supreme Court’s judgment in Shreya Singhal (2015), which relied on safeguards in Section 69A: written reasons, a hearing, and a review. Ms. Kazmi’s notice named no agency, order, or reference number.

First, refuse silence. On October 15, the Internet Freedom Foundation helped four reporters send legal notices to Meta; their accounts were restored within days. Second, demand the takedown orders. MeitY should publish every order, or at least its issuer, date and grounds; Meta should also do the same. Cockroaches can survive anywhere. But a democracy should not need its citizens to be indestructible. It must let them speak.
