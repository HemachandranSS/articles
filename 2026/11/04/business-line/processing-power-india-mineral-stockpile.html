STRATEGIC USE · CRITICAL MINERALS

Processing power key to India’s mineral stockpile

India must not only secure the materials its industries need but also have the ability to turn those materials into products

Nishant Sahdev

Physicist, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill; contributing opinion writer, Wall Street Journal  |  November 4, 2026

What happens when a country has the raw materials its industries need but cannot turn them into the components its factories use? India’s proposed strategic stockpile of critical minerals brings that question into focus. The policy aims to build reserves for electric vehicles, batteries, defence, electronics and energy infrastructure — but stockpiling ingredients is not the same as securing manufacturing capability.

Consider rare-earth permanent magnets. Value lies in composition, processing and finished properties — not merely in owning oxides. Between extraction and a working magnet lies a chain of industrial capabilities warehouses cannot replicate.

Rare-earth scheme

India has approved a ₹7,280-crore scheme for 6,000 tonnes a year of integrated rare-earth permanent-magnet capacity, from oxides to finished magnets. A shortage of a small component can idle an entire EV line: lost revenue exceeds the component’s price. Reserves must match grades, chemical forms and processed materials manufacturers actually use.

Policy should distinguish three objectives: securing mineral supplies, building domestic processing, and ensuring access to finished industrial inputs. They overlap but need different investments, technology and partnerships. Public money tied up in commodities whose prices move before use may not remove the production bottleneck if domestic processors or expertise are absent.

The objective should not be to manufacture everything at home. That would be expensive and, in some cases, impractical. Strategic autonomy means being able to withstand a supply shock without losing control of essential economic activity.

Use of reserves

Rules must spell out which industries access reserves during shortages, how releases are sized, and whether stocks hold raw or processed material. The reserve’s value is the disruption it can absorb, not tonnes announced. Procurement must not compete with private buyers and raise input costs without improving security. Processing, recycling and import diversification remain necessary; neither is instant.

When disruption arrives, the test is simple: can Indian manufacturers keep producing, or do they still depend overseas for processing that makes minerals useful? India must secure materials — and the ability to turn them into products.
