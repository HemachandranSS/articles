INTERNATIONAL RELATIONS · INDIA–CHINA BORDER

On the Arunachal frontier, silence is not a strategy

India’s silence on Chinese border intrusions risks emboldening Beijing and undermining New Delhi’s position

Ashok K. Kantha

Subhas Chandra Bose Chair Professor of International Relations, Chanakya University, Bengaluru; former Ambassador of India to China

For some time, reports have circulated of transgressions by China’s People’s Liberation Army (PLA) in the Asaphila, Taksing and Longju areas of Arunachal Pradesh. The facts are neither clear nor officially acknowledged. Yet, there are signals that something out of the ordinary is happening along this stretch of the border. The matter warrants close attention.

The clearest signal came on October 22, 2026. Speaking at an Indo-Tibetan Border Police (ITBP) function, Director General Shatrujeet Singh Kapoor said: “In the past few days, a situation arose where face-off happened at several locations in Arunachal and it still continues.” This was the first official confirmation of the scale of the problem. This daily reported, citing “government sources”, that the two sides had been in a stand-off at Asaphila, near Taksing, since late July.

Official responses, several questions

Neither the Ministry of External Affairs nor the Chinese Foreign Ministry has denied the face-offs. On October 23, an MEA spokesperson referred questions on operational details to the Ministry of Defence. On October 26, a Chinese counterpart described border patrols as normal. Arunachal Pradesh Chief Minister Pema Khandu stated on October 29 that transgressions were occurring. The Ministry has also linked the situation to differing patrolling patterns. The stand-off has continued through existing dialogue channels — including the Working Mechanism for Consultation and Coordination on India-China Border Affairs, the Special Representatives, and Corps Commanders — even as India prepared to host the BRICS summit in September 2026. The sequence leaves the public narrative shaped as much by unofficial briefings and Beijing’s statements as by a single, authoritative Indian account.

Deeper intrusions, presence in new areas, or obstruction of Indian patrols — as local groups have suggested — would mark a serious, though not altogether unexpected, development in the Eastern Sector. Earlier, adverse Chinese possession south of the boundary was largely confined to pockets in the Namkha Chu and Sumdorong Chu valleys, and at Longju. India should then raise such developments firmly at the diplomatic and, if needed, political level.

India’s silence on Chinese border intrusions risks emboldening Beijing and undermining New Delhi’s position.

China’s claims, India’s rejection

Chinese commentators now speak of enforcing the “1959 Line” in the Eastern Sector. India has never accepted this line. It rests on an unscientific transposing of coordinates from 1914 maps onto present-day maps, disregarding the highest-watershed principle behind the McMahon alignment. In negotiations leading to the 1993 Agreement on the Maintenance of Peace and Tranquility along the Line of Actual Control, India accepted the concept of the LAC but specifically rejected a Chinese notion of a 1959 line. The LAC is the line of actual control on the ground. The 2005 Agreement on Political Parameters and Guiding Principles requires the two sides to take into account “the actual state of border areas” (Article V).

China defines the LAC in terms of sovereignty, yet India keeps referring to “different perceptions” of the LAC. For India, there should be one perception: its own. Formalising an “improved understanding” of the LAC would help China wriggle out of its 1996 commitment to a “common understanding” through exchange of maps — a step Delhi has long sought without adequate reciprocity.

Hiding intrusions weakens border security

Not every transgression need be publicised in operational detail. But when intrusions spill into new areas, adverse possession expands, or patrols are obstructed, the situation becomes far more serious. This was the litmus test applied in the past. Wangdung in 1986 was made public and discussed in Parliament. In Chumar in 2014, the Prime Minister raised the issue with President Xi on the first day of his visit, which led to restoration of the status quo ante. In Eastern Ladakh, the status quo ante of April 2020 is yet to be restored — but Parliament was briefed in 2020, in contrast with the prolonged official quiet around Asaphila after July 2026.

India protests loudly when China assigns invented names to places in Arunachal, yet says little on record about troops on the ground. Denials, obfuscation or conflicting signals encourage more salami-slicing. Candour — acknowledging serious transgressions while protecting tactical detail — strengthens New Delhi’s hand in dealing with Beijing; silence is not a strategy.
