How students are the first line of resistance

N. Sai Balaji

Former AISA President and CPI(ML) activist.

Lead Article  |  November 10, 2026

It would be a grievously wrong reading of the current national climate if one were to see the recent student protests as isolated incidents of resistance. Such a reading assumes that this movement is a temporary expression of dissatisfaction that will eventually dissipate. The movement of today is built on the sediments of the grievances of the past, which were ignored and buried under layers of distortion.

The first section of society that recognises when democracy is under threat and stands up for it are always students. Almost a year after the Narendra Modi-led government first came to power in 2014, students of the Film and Television Institute of India (FTII) went on a hunger strike for 139 days, opposing the appointment of Gajendra Chauhan as chairman of the institute. Mr. Chauhan’s lack of academic credentials was made up for by his proximity to the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh (RSS) and the Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP). These protests, from June-October 2015, were the first act of resistance. The government responded by dismissing the protesters as anti-nationals, an overused modus operandi to counter any protest challenging the government. While Mr. Chauhan wasn’t sacked, his tenure was cut short to 14 months. The government’s victory was temporary.

Agitations of the past

Such grievances cannot be suppressed by distortion—a lesson that governments find hard to understand. If not addressed and resolved, they simply change shape and return in other forms; this is what we have seen time and again. The ‘Occupy UGC’ movement demanding the restoration and a hike of the non-NET fellowship; the ‘Justice for Rohith Vemula’ campaign; the protests against the manufactured sedition cases against students of Jawaharlal Nehru University; the #WhereIsNajeeb movement; the anti-fee hike protests in various universities across the country, including Panjab University in Chandigarh, JNU and Delhi University; and the equal citizenship movement—all are examples of the same. Social and gender justice movements in India such as the 2012 Nirbhaya protests solidify the idea that the youth have the courage to mobilise, diagnose, and remedy society.

The COVID-19 pandemic and subsequent lockdowns witnessed a further attack on the integrity of educational systems. The government imposed the new National Education Policy (NEP) 2020 on students, teachers, and the academic community through a press conference. While the long-term consequences of this policy on the educational system are yet to be seen, issues have already begun to trickle in. Consolidation of power, centralisation of policy, and corruption are the essence of the system that NEP 2020 has built. Students, once again, were the first to resist this.

Students also participated in the protests against the Unnao rape case, the Hathras gang rape, and the sexual harassment faced by wrestlers under the aegis of the Wrestling Federation of India (WFI). They continued to brave police brutality, arbitrary FIRs, and the persistent avoidance of the government in dealing with said issues. The entire country turned a blind eye to the brutality unleashed on students, consumed by the methodical madness of hate which has been moulded into acceptance by the ‘Corporate Raj’ that is, the Adani-Ambanification of India. Linguistic tools, therefore, were insufficient in resisting and challenging this juggernaut of fascist practices.

The sea of humanity that has turned up for the recent protests has been fed through the tributaries of agitations of the past; on the sediment of grievances that have settled over the years. It is ironic that a government that brags about and celebrates its role in the anti-Emergency protests has forgotten that it too began with a student movement against a fee hike in a university in Gujarat. It is presumptuous of the government to imagine that it is unique and will remain insulated from protests.

Different mode of protest

The political participation of Gen Z has brought a shift in how protests are carried out. Gen Z was a storm that rained memes and reels on the political landscape, with satire and non-conformity as a mode of politics; having fun was political resistance. Since the post-COVID era, and especially during the third term of the Modi-led government, a brief survey of social media reveals that platforms such as Instagram and Reddit, along with youth-run YouTube channels, have become sites of resistance and challenge.

It is only natural that the social media-savvy Gen Z grew tired of the WhatsApp-forward drivel of older generations and brought its own rules of engagement with politics into the picture. For Gen Z, protests have become a site for content creation, and the police have become a destabilised collaborative figure in humorous reels, which reflects a refusal to fear the police state. Armed with a no-holds-barred approach, Gen Z has easily dismantled the curated PR caricatures political leaders had built of themselves with thousands of crores.

Interconnected struggles

A few political observers have said that the protests against electoral malpractices are not as emotive an issue as the anti-paper leak agitation. Again, this is an erroneous reading. The anti-SIR protests are yet another powerful turn in the student movement. The youth have many grievances for which to hold the government accountable such as corporate monopoly over jobs, the rise of AI and its regulation, the government’s withdrawal from the public sector, and the consequences of global conflict caused by the U.S.-Israel nexus. Free and fair elections are where they can act on their political aspirations and grievances. However, if the electoral system itself is broken and rigged, the youth can only rely on peaceful protests to seek accountability.

The government had given free rein to the police from day one. Those who opposed Mr. Chauhan and those who stood against former education minister Dharmendra Pradhan all faced lathis and FIRs. And now, peace bonds are being pushed on protesters to kill the democratic uprising. However, there is resistance here too; Neha Bora, AISA President, had recently refused to sign a peace bond, showing how resistance can turn into civil disobedience. To imagine that the government can, through brute force, slam the lid shut on protests is delusional. Let’s not forget how the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967 has been weaponised to silence and jail student and pro-democracy activists.

By denotifying Jantar Mantar as a site of protest, the Modi-led government has denotified democracy. The only course to reclaim this is for India as a whole to become Jantar Mantar through peaceful protests. The protests across various campuses in India demanding the restoration of student union elections are a step towards rebuilding democracy that was destroyed over years of anti-democratic consolidation in our institutions.

“In a country, and more broadly a society, that bleeds with hate, the youth continue to resist with humour and hope.”

You will find the youth protesting, and you will find them in reels. The youth refuse to give up on either of these spaces, displaying the public conscience in ways we never imagined. They are the public health centres of democracy; they diagnose threats and build resistance from the first instance. The responsibility to strengthen this fight falls on other sections of society. The process for democratic restoration has just begun.
