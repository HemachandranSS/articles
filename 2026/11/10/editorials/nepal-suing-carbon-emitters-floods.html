A case for Nepal: suing major carbon emitters for floods

Manoj Kumar Sinha & Anwar Sadat

V-C of Dharmashastra National Law University and president of ISIL; and Associate Professor in international environmental law at ISIL.

Op-Ed  |  November 10, 2026

The catastrophic floods in Nepal which resulted in the deaths of thousands of people in August 2026, is overwhelmingly attributed to climate change. This is yet another worst-case climate change scenario from South Asia, after the floods in Pakistan in 2022.

A debate that has not drawn much attention so far in this part of the world is that there is scope to explore and establish the flood victims as victims of climate wrongs. The case for the Nepalese people needs to be analysed from the prism of climate litigations used by plaintiffs in many jurisdictions of Europe and the U.S. in the last two decades. These plaintiffs, who were exposed to climate-related risks, have brought claims against state and private corporations on the ground of serious risks to life, well-being, health, property, privacy and disruption of family life. Though the plaintiffs have not turned out to be winners in these proceedings every time, the challenge itself has been eye-opening.

Legal reasoning

In a strict legal sense, it is extremely difficult to establish causation between a defendant’s (such as a state or private corporation living miles away) failure to take appropriate measures restricting the emission of Greenhouse Gases (GHG) and the flood victims in those districts of Nepal bordering Tibet.

“While climate wrongs harm society, they do not harm individuals in a sufficiently direct and proximate way.”

The logic that is widely accepted now is that the failure to take appropriate measures by an entity makes an incremental contribution to global cumulative GHGs, which diffuse and leave global and lingering effects on the likelihood of adverse physical events. The impact of such events is itself dependent on extraneous factors such as exposure, vulnerability and resilience. The identification of the victims exposed to risks in contrast to the ‘general’ population is needed to describe that specific group as victims of climate change. This gives them the standing to sue—to justify their invocation of individual or group rights and consider claims for compensation.

The people living in the Tibet-Nepal border area, specifically those living in the Trishuli and Bhote Koshi river basins area of Nepal, have been exposed to Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOFs) over the last three decades. Scholarship focusing on the Tibetan Autonomous Region is of the view that since 2010 both the area and the number of glaciers is retreating at a faster rate than before. Climate scientists predict that the mountain glaciers will lose most of their volume by the end of the 21st century, placing alpine ecosystems and human population at risk. These climate-linked physical events, which were present prior to the flood, could possibly fulfil the normative threshold of the imminence of a catastrophe.

Holding emitters accountable

The case that merits attention in this context is the Lliuya vs RWE case which established that it is possible to causally link the GHG emissions of one large emitter to a concrete risk of life and property in a country that is 5,000 miles away. In the case, Lliuya, a Peruvian national, sued RWE (a German energy provider company headquartered in Germany) under a German private nuisance law on the ground that the company’s past and present coal-fired power generation is responsible for huge quantities of CO2, which reduced the stability of glaciers near his home.

In an important judgment, the Higher Court of Appeal in Hamm on May 28, 2025 ruled that RWE can be held legally responsible in principle, though the specific claim was dismissed due to inadequate proof of an imminent risk. The court accepted that past individual climate harms can proportionally contribute to climate risks and it also upheld that German civil law can apply to transboundary climate harms. This, in effect, amounts to saying that long distance and global scale do not automatically shield a corporation from liability.

In Milieudefensie et al vs Royal Dutch Shell (November, 2024), the Dutch Court of appeal confirmed under Dutch tort law principles that companies which contribute significantly to climate change and have it within their power to contribute to combating it, have an obligation, as per the general duty of care under Dutch law and human rights standards, to limit GHG emissions to counter climate change.

The climate lawsuit brought by four Indonesian islanders against Holcim, a Swiss building material giant, in the Swiss Cantonal Court of Zug (Asmania vs Holcim) is also quite relevant. Here, the plaintiffs sought a reduction of GHGs as well as compensation for present and future damages against Holcim’s excessive CO2 emissions.

Although there is no rule of binding precedent, it is still advisable for the flood victims of Nepal to use the channel of litigations against major GHG emitters, as there is growing judicial awareness of the duties owed by large emitters in responding to the threat of climate change.
