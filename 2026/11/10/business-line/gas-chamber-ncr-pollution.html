Curbing NCR pollution requires long, short term strategies

Editorial Desk

The Hindu Business Line  |  November 10, 2026

A familiar sense of dismay and déjà vu settles in and around Delhi-NCR every winter as suffocating air pollution grips the region. Air pollution has remained hazardous this season with Delhi’s Air Quality Index (AQI) scaling 370—a day after massive firecracker explosions during Diwali. This places it on average in the ‘Very Poor’ category with several stations recording levels much above the ‘Severe’ threshold of 401.

More tellingly, fine particulate matter (PM2.5) was the dominant pollutant at 47 of Delhi’s 49 air pollution monitoring stations. Mumbai’s AQI was less than half of Delhi’s at 172, Bengaluru’s was at 124, Chennai’s was 214, Hyderabad’s was at 122 and Thiruvananthapuram’s was at 66. Meanwhile, Delhi-NCR is in the grip of a public health emergency with a rise in respiratory health ailments.

Seasonal pollution spike from bad weather, stubble burning, vehicles, industry and Diwali firecrackers is well understood, yet governments rely solely on emergency action hand-aids like Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP), instead of tackling root causes. Typically this year, the Capital city has rolled out GRAP Stage III restrictions across the NCR since November 6. The curbs include a ban on BS-III petrol and BS-IV diesel or older four-wheelers in Delhi and five high-density NCR districts, restrictions on older diesel goods vehicles, and suspension of non-essential construction and demolition activities.

The strategy betrays a fundamental failure to recognise that air pollution is a regional, not a municipal, problem. It makes arbitrary distinctions based on vehicle registration, barring BS-IV petrol vehicles registered outside Delhi while allowing identical vehicles registered within the city. Compare this to China’s strategy and success after the severe smog crisis of 2013 which treated clean air as a national economic priority, rather than relying on temporary restrictions whenever pollution reached crisis levels. China targeted pollution at source by tightening emission standards for power plants and industries, phasing out obsolete and highly polluting factories and boilers, rationalising coal consumption and promoting cleaner fuels and renewable energy. It coordinated emission-control measures across Beijing, Tianjin and the industrial province of Hebei.

Delhi has made considerable advances in electrifying the state transport bus fleet, its metro and pushing green taxis. This should be replicated across NCR. Long term air quality management requires an integration of fleet-wide efficiency targets, envisaged in the Corporate Average Fuel Economy norms, with public transit expansion and environment-friendly agricultural and industrial policies. In the short term, bringing back the odd-even rule for vehicles, expansion of public transport and better coordination between States on stubble-burning would serve the region well.
