The economics behind fixing air fares

While the degree of competition is a key factor, airlines also seek to maximise aircraft use through pricing strategies that involve cross-subsidisation between routes.

TCA Srinivasa Raghavan

The Hindu Business Line  |  November 10, 2026

Between 1998 and 2022 I worked as a consultant at the Asian Institute of Transport, Delhi. What we detested most there was the highly annoying and boring debate over air fares every winter. People who could afford to fly started grumbling about the high air fares in the festival season. Socialists then demanded government intervention to cap fares because they suspected price gouging. And so on, unfailingly, and very drearily, every winter until end-January.

But despite the high fares, the planes have always gone full. The airlines have always said that prices go up because the demand for seats outstrips their supply. And, when they can, they have also pointed to high fuel prices and other rising costs. Aviation fuel taxation has been a pet peeve.

This is happening again now. So what actually is the problem? This annual tamasha happens because it’s one of those situations where the passengers and the airlines are both right and much can be said by both sides.

But this kind of periodic fluctuation in prices is an inherent feature of any business that operates networks. It doesn’t matter what the network is, telecommunications, electricity, railways, roads, etc., prices will fluctuate around a rising trend line, usually a little bit but sometimes a lot.

The market structure makes a big difference because the economics of networks is completely different from normal factory or services output economics. To start with, there is strong cross-subsidisation between different segments of the network. Then there is the way assets are used. Then come the pricing issues. And so on.

Network pricing

The point about network pricing or, to give it the correct technical name, Ramsey pricing, is that a network seeks to maximise revenue from the entire network rather than profits on each segment. Revenue maximisation has only one objective: to use assets most efficiently. A plane or a wagon or a truck or a ship incurs deadweight losses if it’s not carrying people or things.

The Ramsey rule requires pricing to be based on the price elasticity of demand. The less this is, the higher the price increase. This is what explains the price structure on different routes.

Its aim, as Anthony Sampson wrote four decades ago in his classic book on aviation, Empires of the Sky, is to “put bottoms on seats”. Maximising aircraft use then requires pricing strategies that use cross-subsidisation between routes. But there are two options here. The low traffic routes can subsidise the high traffic ones or vice versa.

Very often they make no sense at all as when fares on short flights seem to cost a lot more than seems intuitively reasonable. Suffice it to say average costs on the network hold the key.

The degree of competition

How can price gouging be managed or prevented? As always, the answer lies in the degree of competition. And the dismaying truth is that India has never been able to manage it. So over the last 30 years we have gone from having over a dozen airlines to just two now.

Officially there are nine airlines but seven of these don’t really matter because they have so few aircraft and routes. They can’t influence fares. The policy question, therefore, is which to have: small, medium or large oligopolies. Far from addressing itself to this basic question, the government has gone to sleep at the wheel. So what we have now is a duopoly, which is the worst form of competition.

But how does a government enable another airline to be started or if it’s already there, persuade it to become bigger? The answer depends on the level of profits that the duopolists are making. If these are large enough, new entrants should get attracted. That’s why one of our two duopolists is a sitting duck. Someone with deep pockets should take it on. If operating costs are a problem, owning a few major airports would be of great help.

  1. Ramsey Pricing on Route Networks (வலையமைப்பு விலை)
  2. Cross-Subsidisation Between Routes (பாதை குறுக்கு மானியம்)
  3. Aviation Duopoly in India (இரட்டை ஏர்லைன் சூழல்)
  4. Asset-Use Maximisation (விமானப் பயன்பாடு)
