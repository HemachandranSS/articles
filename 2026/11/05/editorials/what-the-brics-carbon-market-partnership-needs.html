What the BRICS carbon market partnership needs

BRICS can turn fragmented carbon markets into a coordinated trading framework

Shashank Pandey

1991 Fellow, Mercatus Center at George Mason University; works on carbon market operations in Uttar Pradesh

On September 12, at the 18th BRICS Summit in New Delhi, the New Delhi Declaration confirmed that BRICS members look forward to implementing the Memorandum of Understanding on the BRICS Carbon Markets Partnership. The commitment remains modest: on capacity-building and experience-sharing, with no trading framework, targets or timeline. The Rio summit acknowledged the memorandum in 2025, and New Delhi has reiterated it. With India’s BRICS presidency entering its final quarter, operationalising the partnership requires not so much a common trading platform as a common rulebook.

Eight BRICS members reportedly operate voluntary markets, and Brazil, China, India and Indonesia host over a third of the carbon credit projects registered worldwide. Compliance systems are maturing, too. China’s emissions trading system (ETS) added steel, cement and aluminium through its 2024–25 allocation plan. India’s Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS) has notified emission-intensity targets for 490 entities across nine sectors and opened an offset mechanism. Brazil’s Law of 2024 establishes the Brazilian Greenhouse Gas Emissions Trading System (SBCE), a cap-and-trade system. However, none of these markets is linked to another.

Divergence and a common framework

The same tonne of abatement is a different legal instrument in each jurisdiction. China and India allocate against output-based intensity benchmarks; Brazil sets an absolute cap. Offset rules look inward; the carbon unit sits under a securities regulator in Brazil but trades on power exchanges under Central Electricity Regulatory Commission regulations in India. The pressure to align is partly external. The European Union’s Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) entered its definitive phase in January 2026; the United Kingdom’s carbon border mechanism follows in January 2027. Both cover iron and steel, aluminium, cement, fertilisers and hydrogen — important export lines for several BRICS members. A carbon price paid at origin is credited only if supported by verified evidence; exporters without credible data face conservative default values. The U.K.’s recognition of India’s CCTS in August 2026 shows the pattern: relief depends on paperwork completed by an ISO 14065-accredited verifier, and each exporting country has negotiated on its own. Objections to CBAM carry more weight when backed by a credible accounting standard.

BRICS can turn fragmented carbon markets into a coordinated trading framework.

Linking domestic carbon projects

A common framework for measurement, reporting and verification (MRV) should be the partnership’s first deliverable. Common monitoring rules consistent with ISO 14064 for sectors exposed to border adjustments, mutual recognition of verifiers accredited by national accreditation bodies, and harmonised registry data fields so credits can be tracked across systems would give exporters a single compliance pathway. This builds on the BRICS 2025 Principles for Fair, Inclusive and Transparent Carbon Accounting. Full market linkage is neither likely nor desirable — intensity-based and cap-based systems are hard to link without one side diluting its design — but agreement on how credits are measured and checked is useful even if compliance markets never merge.

The second step is a small Paris Agreement Article 6.2 pilot: two or three members could recognise one another’s methodologies where each has approved methods and accept a capped volume of partner-registry units within domestic offset limits. India already has a Joint Crediting Mechanism with Japan under Article 6.2. Corresponding adjustments mean the host country cannot count a sold reduction towards its own nationally determined contribution — a real cost for developing countries with ambitious targets. Cross-border settlement raises capital-account questions. Registry integrity requires serial numbers and retirement data to prevent double issuance. The pilot’s value lies less in volume traded than in turning each friction into a concrete drafting question with an institutional owner.

Using existing institutions

The New Development Bank (NDB) could host a shared registry or a purchasing facility for pilot units. The BRICS dialogue on aligning carbon markets with adaptation could channel part of intra-BRICS transfer proceeds to adaptation, as Article 6.4 of the Paris Agreement already does for the UN crediting mechanism. China’s scale would make it the price-setter in any fully linked market — a concern for smaller members and for India. Several members compete in the same steel and aluminium export markets, which limits willingness to share data or align benchmarks. Still, common MRV and a methodology-level pilot are low-cost, preserve regulatory autonomy, strengthen India’s hand in CBAM negotiations through collective standards rather than one-by-one bargaining, and support updated climate pledges consistent with the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities. India’s presidency has months left to adopt monitoring rules, run a capped pilot with registry exchange, keep NDC accounting safe through corresponding adjustments, and pursue bilateral recognition with the EU and U.K. in parallel rather than waiting for bloc consensus.
