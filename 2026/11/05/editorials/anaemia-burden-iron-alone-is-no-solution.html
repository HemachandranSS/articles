Anaemia burden — iron alone is no solution

India must tackle its anaemia burden through targeted nutrition, better diagnostics and comprehensive public health measures, not salt fortification alone

Chandrakant Lahariya

Practising physician; author of Pill-Free: You Don’t Need Everything You Have Been Prescribed

India’s anaemia burden is not in doubt. The fifth National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5) found that more than half of women and about two-thirds of young children are anaemic. In this context, double-fortified salt (DFS) — fortified with iodine and iron — is often proposed as a potentially transformative solution. However, the case for DFS rests on convenience: everyone consumes salt, so why not add iron to it? But convenience is not efficacy, and a policy that asks the country to continue consuming salt to deliver iron deserves closer scrutiny.

Iron fortification of salt is a vehicle that cuts against public health. Indians consume, by most estimates, roughly twice the five grams of salt a day recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO). About one in three adults has hypertension, and India’s own non-communicable disease (NCD) action plan commits to a 30% relative reduction in salt intake. DFS works only if households keep eating a steady, generous amount of salt — the opposite of the message for blood-pressure control. If people cut salt, they cut iron; if DFS is to deliver enough iron, salt intake must stay high. One programme succeeds only if the other fails. WHO itself says salt reduction and salt fortification must be coordinated closely so nutrient intake stays adequate while hypertension risk falls — citing general support for fortification without that condition captures only half its position.

Iron is not a harmless nutrient

The body has no regulated pathway to excrete excess iron; accumulation can fuel oxidative stress. The Pemba trial in Tanzania (2006) linked iron and folic acid supplementation among children in a malaria-endemic area to more hospital admissions and deaths. A Kenyan study (2015) found that iron-fortified foods shifted infants’ gut microbiota towards harmful bacteria and increased diarrhoea — much unabsorbed iron reaches the colon and can feed pathogens. India carries millions of beta-thalassaemia carriers and a high burden of sickle cell disease in tribal belts; people with thalassaemia on regular transfusions are already prone to iron overload. Universal screening before mass fortification through channels that reach hundreds of millions is not realistic. Iron in DFS is non-haem iron; the typical Indian meal works against it. Phytates in grains and pulses, polyphenols in tea and coffee, and calcium in milk inhibit uptake; vitamin C with the meal helps. During infection or inflammation, hepcidin rises and blocks iron uptake — so fortification works least where infection burdens are highest. Cooking losses and uneven dosing at home are rarely captured in supervised efficacy trials.

India must tackle its anaemia burden through targeted nutrition, better diagnostics and comprehensive public health measures, not salt fortification alone.

Iron deficiency only in half of anaemia cases

Anaemia has multiple causes and cannot be solved by an additive in the salt bottle. Globally, only about half of anaemia is attributed to iron deficiency. India’s Comprehensive National Nutrition Survey, which measured biomarkers, found that iron deficiency explained only part of anaemia among children and adolescents. Folate and vitamin B12 shortfalls, chronic infections such as hookworm and malaria, poor sanitation, and inherited haemoglobinopathies all contribute. Haemoglobin-only screening cannot tell planners which tool failed. Iron already reaches people through weekly iron–folic acid supplementation, fortified rice in the public distribution system, mid-day meals and multiple other programmes; adding DFS without tracking cumulative intake risks “stacking” — several channels dosing the same child with no published assessment of total exposure. Salt also distributes iron by how much people eat, not by need: adult men tend to consume more salt than young children and women of reproductive age — the groups worst hit by anaemia — while hypertensive adults are told to cut the very carrier of the iron.

A better pathway

The Anaemia Mukt Bharat strategy under POSHAN Abhiyaan already targets six population groups with six interventions and six institutional mechanisms — a platform for targeted delivery rather than a single population-wide salt fix. The government should run salt reduction and fortification as one coordinated plan, deliver iron through programmes aimed at high-burden groups, and fund diagnostics that distinguish iron deficiency from other causes before mass dosing. Deworming, malaria control, water and sanitation, and affordable dietary diversity matter as much as tablets. Where fortification fits local food habits, better-absorbed forms such as NaFeEDTA — as used in Vietnam’s fish sauce programme — may have a role, but only after district pilots that measure ferritin, haemoglobin and inflammation, and track possible harm in malaria-endemic and haemoglobinopathy-prone areas. DFS may remain one layer within that architecture, not a central flagship justified by convenience alone. Promotion must never suggest that “double fortified” salt makes eating more salt healthy; the WHO’s five-gram limit belongs beside every label. Success should be judged by haemoglobin outcomes and cause-specific correction, not by how many fortified bags were distributed.
