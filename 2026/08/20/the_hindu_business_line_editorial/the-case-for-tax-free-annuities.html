OPINION / TAXATION & PUBLIC FINANCE

The case for tax-free annuities

A retiree’s income, secured through an annuity, is less likely to require emergency state support in old age.

By TC Suseel Kumar & R Sudhakar

There is a fundamental paradox at the heart of India’s public finance framework: the Union Government waives income tax for investors who lend it money for five years, yet levies lifetime slab-rate taxes on retirees who commit their savings for decades.

Consider Section 85 of the Income-Tax Act, 2025. A property seller with a large capital gain can secure a complete tax exemption up to ₹50 lakh by investing in five-year infrastructure bonds issued by entities such as REC, PFC, HUDCO and IRFC. The logic is clear: the sovereign forgoes short-term tax revenue to secure low-cost, directed capital for national infrastructure. A decade ago, tax-free infrastructure bonds under Section 10(15)(iv)(h) used the same model to channel retail savings into highways and railways.

Yet when an Indian retiree converts life savings into an annuity — providing regulated, long-duration capital that ultimately flows substantially into government securities — the sovereign offers no comparable income-tax relief. Instead, periodic annuity income is taxed at slab rates for life. India has left a valuable economic trade half-made.

The GST Council took an important first step on September 22, 2025, by exempting individual life insurance and annuity premiums from the GST. But the more consequential issue remains: income tax on the annuity payout.

Where the corpus goes
Under the National Pension System (NPS), contributions accumulate in market-linked funds with tax advantages. At retirement, up to 60 per cent of the corpus can be withdrawn tax-free, while the balance purchases an annuity from a PFRDA-empanelled provider, with the purchase itself exempt.

PFRDA regulations notified in December 2025 reduced mandatory annuitisation for non-government subscribers on normal exit from 40 per cent to 20 per cent, and removed it entirely for a corpus of ₹8 lakh or less. However, because the lump-sum exemption remains capped at 60 per cent, the additional 20 per cent now withdrawable is taxed at slab rates. Pension regulation has moved faster than the tax code.

Once the corpus reaches an insurer, IRDAI investment rules direct a substantial share into government securities and State Development Loans. Legally, the retiree owns an insurance contract; economically, the arrangement channels long-duration savings into sovereign debt through an insurer.

The contrast is striking. The tax-free bond investor receives an exemption for financing short-term state-directed borrowing. The annuity provider provides vastly longer-duration capital and pays full slab-rate tax on the resulting income.

The case for annuities extends beyond tax parity. As India ages, household financial fragility in retirement will become a growing social-security challenge. Mutual-fund systematic withdrawal plans, equity portfolios and fixed deposits expose retirees to market volatility, reinvestment risk and the possibility of exhausting their capital.

Annuities uniquely transfer longevity risk to a regulated financial institution. Through mortality pooling, they guarantee income for as long as the annuitant lives.

In retirement-planning discussions, annuities are routinely absent since tax-conscious advisers have little incentive to recommend a product whose payouts are taxed at slab rates. Outside the NPS, the position is worse. That corpus is already-taxed household savings, and Indian law has no exclusion ratio, splitting each payout into capital returned and interest earned. Part of every taxed payment is the buyer’s own capital, taxed twice.

The proposal
The predictable objection from the Ministry of Finance and CBDT could be that NPS subscribers already enjoy substantial tax benefits and that exempting annuity payouts would create excessive tax shelter.

But this overlooks the annuity’s economic function. Its income is substantially generated by the insurer’s deployment of funds into long-duration debt, including government securities. Taxing the payout therefore taxes the return generated by capital that is simultaneously financing the sovereign.

More importantly, the annuity combines three public objectives: mobilising long-term capital, transferring longevity risk away from households and reducing potential future dependence on state-supported old-age welfare.

Parliament should therefore provide that annual annuity income up to ₹12 lakh be excluded from total income for regulated lifetime annuity products issued by IRDAI-registered insurers, with a flat 10 per cent final withholding tax on the excess.

To prevent arbitrage, the concession should apply only to lifetime annuities purchased at or after superannuation, including variants that return the purchase price to nominees on death. Deferred accumulation products, surrender-heavy wealth products and disguised tax-free bonds should remain outside the regime. Insurers should report exempt payouts through PAN-based tracking.

The direct revenue sacrifice would be real, but the wider fiscal economics could be favourable. If tax parity expands the retail annuity market, additional institutional capital flowing into long-dated central government securities and SDLs could conservatively reach ₹40,000–50,000 crore annually. Even a modest 50-basis-point reduction in sovereign borrowing costs would generate recurring interest savings as the debt stock grows.

There is also a less visible fiscal benefit. Every retiree whose lifetime income is secured through an annuity is less vulnerable to exhausting savings and requiring emergency state support in old age. That reduction in future welfare liabilities has significant long-term economic value.

Implementation should be sequenced carefully. IRDAI should mandate inflation-linked annuity options, strengthen distributor controls and prescribe standardised yield disclosures. PFRDA should build a transparent platform for comparing annuity rates. Tax-deduction-at-source rules must also be aligned so that an exempt payment is not first taxed and only later reclaimed.

India has already demonstrated that it is willing to exchange tax revenue for strategic capital. Capital-gains bonds and tax-free infrastructure bonds used precisely this mechanism: revenue foregone today in return for stable, directed financing of national priorities.

The annuity offers the same trade at greater scale and longer duration, while addressing an equally urgent priority: guaranteed lifetime income for an ageing population. The government has proved twice that it can make this bargain. With the recent GST exemption, it has begun a third. What remains is to complete it: remove income tax on annuity income up to the threshold, put the annuity firmly on every retirement planner’s table, and allow India’s retail savings to fund both its retirees and its nation-building needs.
