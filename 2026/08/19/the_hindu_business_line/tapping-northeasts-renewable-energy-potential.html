OPINION / RENEWABLE ENERGY & POLICY

Tapping Northeast’s renewable energy potential

The region does not lack capital. Its real test is whether transmission, storage and project execution can match generation.

By Rouhin Deb & Nikhil Sinha

The Northeast is emerging as one of the most consequential theatres of India’s renewable-energy transition. Assam has announced a ₹77,353-crore investment roadmap to expand generation, while Arunachal Pradesh is working towards nearly 19 GW of commissioned hydropower capacity against a resource base the state estimates at more than 50 GW. The scale of the ambition is striking. Yet the central question is no longer whether the region can attract capital or announce capacity. It is whether it can build the grid required to convert that capacity into usable economic power.

That distinction matters. The Northeast’s electricity challenge is often folded into the wider story of financially stressed state utilities. But the region’s constraint is of a different order. Unlike states where distribution losses have been aggravated by large subsidy burdens, much of the Northeast’s problem lies in decades of under-built transmission infrastructure, delayed execution and weak capitalisation of projects. In other words, the bottleneck is less about the absence of money than about the ability to move electricity reliably from where it is generated to where it can be consumed.

The gap between potential and utilisation shows how large the unfinished task remains. Only about 4 per cent of the region’s estimated 130 GW renewable potential — including 66 GW of hydropower potential — is currently utilised. The historical record has been one of prolonged delays. More recently, central public-sector undertakings such as NHPC have stepped in to take over distressed state assets and restart stalled construction. This can help revive generation. But generation alone does not create an energy economy.

For that, power must reach new centres of demand. Assam’s ₹27,000-crore Tata Electronics chip-assembly facility, the Sabroom Special Economic Zone in Tripura, and Sikkim’s pharmaceutical manufacturing base are examples of the industrial opportunities that could give the region’s energy build-out an economic multiplier. Their success will depend not merely on installed megawatts but on the quality, stability and reach of the transmission network serving them.

STORAGE CAPACITY
Storage is the other side of the equation. Assam’s Pumped Storage Power Policy, 2025, has approved four projects with a combined capacity of 4,900 MW. Pumped storage can absorb cheap surplus electricity during periods of low demand and release it when demand rises or solar generation falls. In principle, this can give the grid the flexibility needed to integrate more renewable power. In practice, however, pumped storage is only as useful as the transmission system connected to it.

The Northeast also presents unusually demanding engineering conditions. Large parts of the region fall in Seismic Zone V. Pumped-storage projects involving dual reservoirs must therefore contend with risks such as water ingress, slope instability and cave-ins.

The more persistent problem is the gap between sanctioned transmission expenditure and assets actually commissioned on the ground. This is where regulatory reform can make a difference. One option is to link approvals and disbursements more closely to measurable construction milestones and actual capitalisation rather than to proposed expenditure alone. Such a framework would reward delivery, make delays more visible and reduce the distance between utility plans and completed infrastructure.

The Northeast’s energy deficitdeficit therefore cannot be understood simply through the familiar lens of fiscal indiscipline. Its core weakness is infrastructure that has not expanded at the pace demanded by geography, industry and new generation capacity.

There is also a social and strategic dimension to this transition. Large hydro and storage projects in ecologically and politically sensitive areas need more than statutory clearance. Models that give tribal communities a share in financially viable projects can help move them from being petitioners at the edge of decision-making to stakeholders in the assets being created.

Hydropower development in Arunachal Pradesh also has implications beyond electricity markets. Down-river infrastructure and a stronger Indian presence in the Brahmaputra basin intersect with questions of downstream riparian interests and water security at a time when China is expanding upstream dam construction. Energy planning in the Northeast, therefore, sits at the intersection of economic development, federal infrastructure, ecological risk and strategic policy.

The lesson is straightforward. The Northeast does not need generation targets and transmission plans to proceed on separate tracks. They must be built as one system. A gigawatt generated at a riverhead in Arunachal Pradesh or a solar facility in Assam has limited economic value if the grid cannot evacuate it, store it when necessary and deliver it to industry or the national market.
