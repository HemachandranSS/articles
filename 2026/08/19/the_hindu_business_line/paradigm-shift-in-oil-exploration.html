Paradigm shift in oil exploration

Under Samudra Manthan, Centre will go beyond being a regulator to sharing risks with the private sector. Will it work?

By Richa Mishra

Will India get lucky in its third attempt at hydrocarbon hunt through the Cabinet-approved ₹84,084 crore ‘Samudra Manthan’ scheme, or will this just be another expensive mistake? This time, the government is putting its own money on the line. The ‘Samudra Manthan’ or National Offshore Exploration Scheme marks a fundamental shift from India’s traditional policy-reform approach to an active, state-funded, mission-mode framework.

Past regulatory overhauls couldn’t fix India’s deepwater stagnation. The New Exploration Licensing Policy regime relied on complex production-sharing contracts, the existing Hydrocarbon Exploration and Licensing Policy (HELP) and Open Acreage Licensing Policy (OALP) offer fiscal incentives (such as revenue sharing and marketing freedom). But, they leave the massive financial and geological risks of deepwater drilling entirely on the corporate explorers.

Under Samudra Manthan, the government is stepping into the trenches as a financial risk-sharer and infrastructure builder. Under this new blueprint, the government will directly co-fund 50 per cent of high-risk exploratory wells and finance common sub-sea pipelines to unlock India’s ultra-deepwater basins.

UNLOCKING POTENTIAL
According to the government, the Scheme translates the Prime Minister’s vision, articulated from the Red Fort on Independence Day 2025, of a modern-day Samudra Manthan to unlock India’s vast offshore energy potential. Its objective is to strengthen India’s energy security, accelerating domestic exploration and production and promote technological leadership.

The scheme encompasses a comprehensive set of interventions across the offshore exploration value chain. It provides for large-scale acquisition, processing and interpretation of high-quality seismic data, accelerated deepwater and ultra-deepwater exploratory drilling, scientific drilling in frontier basins, development of common offshore production and evacuation infrastructure, and establishment of an integrated oil and gas manufacturing and services zone.

It also includes dedicated provisions for digital programme management, capacity building, technology adoption, stakeholder engagement and international outreach, creating an integrated ecosystem to accelerate offshore exploration and production in the country.

It is expected to catalyse reserve accretion of over 600 Million Metric Tonnes of Oil Equivalent (MMTOE), substantially increase offshore exploration activity, promote higher domestic oil and gas production, generate large-scale employment, strengthen domestic manufacturing under Make in India and Atmanirbhar Bharat, and foster a globally competitive ecosystem for offshore technologies and services. The scheme is also expected to stimulate significant investments across the exploration and production value chain, creating long-term opportunities for industry, innovation and economic growth.

Over the past decade, the government has undertaken transformative reforms across the upstream sector, including opening almost the entire offshore acreage for exploration, modernising the legislative and contractual framework, and strengthening the National Data Repository. Building on these reforms, Samudra Manthan will usher in a new era of accelerated offshore exploration through strategic public investment, cutting-edge technologies and world-class infrastructure.

THE CHALLENGES
But critics say this is a desperate measure by the government to boost India’s hydrocarbon production and attract big players. It is not exactly a PLI scheme but definitely shifts away from production sharing contract regime.

Geopolitical tension was one of the biggest catalysts for the ‘Samudra Manthan’ scheme. It is India’s huge reliance on imported oil and gas that has triggered the government to aggressively pivot toward deep-sea self-reliance.

Though the finer details are still awaited, this scheme will have its set of challenges. These include high geological failure rate or hitting a dry well — the stark reality of deepwater exploration is that three out of every four wells fail to yield commercial discoveries. Since the government is financing up to 50 per cent of the drilling costs, a massive chunk of public money could literally disappear into dry, unviable wells, inviting public and political scrutiny over the use of taxpayer funds, critics fear.

Deepwater and ultra-deepwater drilling require ultra-specialised, high-pressure, low-temperature equipment and sophisticated deep-sea drillships and India lacks a robust domestic manufacturing eco-system for complex sub-sea architecture.

Also deepwater projects typically carry a 5-10-year gestation timeline from initial drilling to the first commercial barrel. So Samudra Manthan will offer no relief to India’s near-term energy import pressures or current domestic production declines.

A bigger challenge is the “Bureaucratic Coordination” for shared clusters. The plan to spend ₹10,000 crore on shared production and evacuation infrastructure is a completely new concept for the Indian exploration and production space.

Managing a single common pipeline network utilised by fierce public and private competitors such as ONGC, Oil India, and Reliance Industries will require incredibly complex tariff-setting, precise volume allocation, and robust legal frameworks to prevent litigation.

Decades of offering just regulatory tax breaks failed because global explorers refused to bear the huge deep-sea dry wells costs alone. Flipping the old playbook on its head, the government has now stepped in as a direct financial risk-sharer. This structural shift from hands-off regulator to active risk-taker is the government’s definitive play to unlock its uncharted maritime exclusive economic zones.
