OPINION · GEOPOLITICS · SUPPLY CHAINS

Many facets of strategic dependence

BEYOND SUPPLY CHAINS. Critical inputs apart, a company’s dependence can extend to tech, IP, and manufacturing know-how

By G Venkat Raman

The current disruption in the Strait of Hormuz is revealing that its significance extends well beyond oil, gas, and petrochemicals. Disruptions to energy, industrial gases and specialised chemical inputs are exposing strategic dependencies in sectors far removed from the Gulf, including semiconductor manufacturing and the industries that depend upon advanced chips.

Hormuz is, therefore, more than an energy and shipping chokepoint. It illustrates how disruption at one point in the global economy can expose strategic dependencies several tiers away from the original source. A company may have no factory, supplier, or customer anywhere near the Gulf and still discover that a critical input, technology, or production capability ultimately depends upon flows affected by it. What appears to be a geographically distant geopolitical risk can therefore become an enterprise-level vulnerability.

This points to a larger challenge for global business. Firms have spent the past several years diversifying suppliers, relocating production and building alternative logistics routes in response to geopolitical risk. These measures can reduce geographical concentration, but they do not necessarily eliminate the strategic dependencies embedded within the wider production network. The real chokepoint may lie several tiers upstream, in a difficult-to-replace technology or capability, or increasingly in data and digital infrastructure.

The question firms increasingly need to ask is therefore not simply: Where is our network located? It is: What does our network ultimately depend upon? Answering that question requires looking beyond conventional supply chains towards the wider architecture of dependence underlying the enterprise. A company may depend on critical inputs, components, energy, and logistics routes. But it may also depend on specialized equipment, intellectual property, software, manufacturing know-how and skills. Increasingly, data, cloud infrastructure, platforms, algorithms, and connectivity constitute another layer of dependence. Strategic dependency boundaries often extend far beyond the legal boundaries of the firm.

Not every dependency, however, constitutes a strategic vulnerability. Four factors help distinguish routine dependencies from potential chokepoints: criticality, concentration, substitutability, and time sensitivity. How critical is the dependency to operations or competitiveness? How concentrated is its supply, capability, or control? How easily and quickly can it be substituted? And how long can the organisation operate before disruption begins to hurt? The more critical and concentrated a dependency is, the more difficult it is to substitute, and the more time-sensitive it becomes, the greater its strategic significance.

“Reducing dependence may increase autonomy and control, but it can also mean higher costs, slower deployment, or loss of access to superior capabilities.”

The difficulty is that some of these dependencies may be invisible to senior management. Firms typically understand their immediate suppliers better than suppliers several tiers upstream. Yet an obscure chemical, industrial gas, piece of equipment or logistics route deep inside a production network can become the point through which geopolitical disruption eventually reaches the firm. A company’s operational footprint and its dependency footprint may therefore look quite different.

Technology makes the problem still more complicated. With a conventional supply dependency, managers ask whether another company can supply the input. With a technological dependency, the deeper question is whether someone else possesses an equivalent capability to produce or replicate what is required. Multiple suppliers therefore do not necessarily represent genuine technological alternatives. A firm may diversify its chip suppliers, for instance, but those suppliers may still depend on ASML for the extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography equipment required to produce the most advanced chips. The apparent diversification of suppliers can therefore conceal concentration in the underlying technological capability.

Semiconductors illustrate the distinction. TSMC’s expanding manufacturing presence in Arizona reduces the geographical concentration of advanced chip production. Yet manufacturing capacity is only one element of the semiconductor ecosystem. Advanced packaging, accumulated process capabilities, specialized skills, and supplier networks also matter. Replicating a fabrication facility is therefore different from replicating the technological ecosystem surrounding it. Geographic diversification does not automatically produce capability diversification.

Difficult Choice
There is another complication. Strategic dependence can sometimes be the source of competitive advantage. Ford’s relationship with China’s CATL illustrates the dilemma. Access to CATL’s battery technology can support the development of US battery manufacturing capability, but the relationship has simultaneously attracted political scrutiny over technological dependence on China. This creates a difficult choice for firms. Reducing dependence may increase autonomy and control, but it can also mean higher costs, slower deployment, or loss of access to superior capabilities. Dependence can therefore create vulnerability and competitive advantage simultaneously.

Strategic dependence can also arise in digital ecosystems, although in a different form. A platform that becomes deeply embedded in a country's information environment can create dependence on digital infrastructure whose critical elements remain externally controlled. TikTok illustrates the problem. Its scale in the US has turned questions about access to user data and ultimate control over its recommendation algorithm into matters of national security. The strategic dependence here lies not in a physical input or production capability, but in reliance on a digital platform whose data and algorithmic architecture may remain outside domestic control. The concern is therefore not merely data privacy. Dependence on a foreign-controlled digital platform can create vulnerabilities involving data access, jurisdiction, and potential influence over the information environment, which can in turn acquire national-security significance.

The managerial response cannot therefore simply be to eliminate dependence. Different chokepoints require different responses. Excessive supplier concentration may justify diversification. Low substitutability may require developing alternatives. Poor upstream visibility requires deeper mapping. Short disruption tolerance may justify buffers and redundancy. Critical technological dependence may require capability-building or partnerships. Data and digital exposure may demand different governance and architectural choices. Some dependencies may simply have to be managed rather than eliminated.

In an era when economic interdependence increasingly intersects with national security, firms therefore need to look beyond the geography of their global footprint. The strategic question is no longer only where the enterprise sources, manufactures, and sells. It is whether management understands the deeper architecture on which all those activities depend. The next frontier of geopolitical risk management may therefore be less about mapping countries and more about mapping dependencies.
